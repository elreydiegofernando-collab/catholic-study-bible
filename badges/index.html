Authority badges

A badge says how firmly the Church has settled one reading of a verse: one sentence that says what the verse means. It goes on that reading, never on the verse as a whole, so one verse can carry several readings with different badges.

Every badge names the Church document behind it, at the exact place, and is set by a person after the sources have been checked. The first approved badges cover John 6, Matthew 16:13-19, John 20, Romans 5:12-21, James 5:13-16 and the words of institution. More will follow as each is reviewed.

The five levels

  • Defined

    The Church settled this with her highest authority: the definition of an ecumenical council, or a pope defining a doctrine for the whole Church. Catholics hold it as part of the faith.

    It needs: A council's canon or decree, or a solemn papal definition, cited at its exact place.

    For example: The Council of Trent (Session XIV, 1551) teaches that in John 20:22-23 Christ gave the apostles and their successors the power to forgive and retain sins.

  • Church teaches

    The Church teaches this reading in the Catechism, a council or papal teaching, without a solemn definition on this exact point. Catholics accept it.

    It needs: A Church document that gives this reading: a Catechism paragraph, a council, an encyclical.

    For example: The Catechism reads Christ's words about eating his flesh in John 6 of the Eucharist.

  • Fathers' common reading

    Many of the early Church Fathers read the verse this way and none of the major Fathers reads it otherwise. Their agreement carries great weight, though it is not binding on its own.

    It needs: The Fathers' own texts, at least three of them, each cited at its place.

    For example: That “the flesh profiteth nothing” (John 6:64) does not mean that Christ's own flesh is of no use.

  • Free opinion

    The Church has not settled this, and it does not touch faith or morals. Catholics may hold either view.

    It needs: A document that leaves the point open, or the general rule together with Fathers or approved writers who read it differently.

    For example: Whether the “days” of Genesis 1 are days of twenty-four hours or longer periods: the Biblical Commission said in 1909 that Catholic scholars may discuss it.

  • Rejected

    The Church has condemned this reading. A short answer, in our own words, says why.

    It needs: The condemning text at its exact place, and the answer.

    For example: That Christ's words in John 20:23 mean only the power to preach the Gospel (Trent, Session XIV, canon 3 on Penance).

Other labels

  • Note

    Some verses also carry a plain note with no badge, for example where the Church has left the reading of a passage open.

  • A teaching tied to the verse

    A card marked “A teaching tied to the verse” puts the badge on a teaching connected with the verse, not on what the verse itself means.

  • Defined elsewhere

    Defined

    A card marked “Defined elsewhere” shows a dogma defined on another text, linked from this verse so you can see where it rests.

Defined is not the only binding level

The First Vatican Council taught that the Church proposes what God has revealed in two ways: by a solemn judgment, or by her ordinary and universal teaching (Dei Filius, chapter 3, 1870). So a reading marked Church teaches can also be a matter of faith. The two badges differ in how the teaching was given, not in whether Catholics may set it aside.

How a badge is decided

  • No badge without a citation: a council's session and canon, a paragraph number, or a page.
  • Each level needs its own kind of citation: a council's definition for Defined, a Church document for Church teaches, at least three Fathers' own texts for Fathers' common reading, a document that leaves the point open for Free opinion, and the condemning text for Rejected.
  • Quotations come only from public-domain translations, checked word for word against the source. Copyrighted documents, such as the Catechism and the Second Vatican Council, are cited by number with a link and a summary in our own words.
  • When a reading could fit two levels, it gets the lower one until the documents show otherwise.
  • Each badge is drafted with AI help from the documents it cites, checked against the printed sources, and approved by the site's owner before it shows.

What the badges do not mean

  • They do not rank how important a verse is.
  • A verse with no badge is not thereby a free question: most verses have simply not been reviewed.
  • Rejected describes a reading the Church condemned, in neutral words. It is not a judgment on any person, or on what any church believes today.

The documents behind the rules

  • Council of Trent, Session IV (1546)

    No one may interpret Scripture, in faith and morals, against the sense the Church holds or against the unanimous consent of the Fathers.

  • First Vatican Council, Dei Filius (1870)

    Renews Trent's rule, and teaches that the Church proposes revealed truth by solemn judgment or by her ordinary and universal teaching.

  • Leo XIII, Providentissimus Deus (1893)

    The Fathers are of the highest authority where they all read a text one way in matters of faith and morals; the sacred writers did not set out to teach natural science.

  • Pontifical Biblical Commission, on Genesis 1-3 (1909)

    Where the Fathers differ and the Church has defined nothing, each may hold his own prudent opinion, saving the Church's judgment.

  • Pius XII, Divino Afflante Spiritu (1943), §47

    Few texts have had their sense fixed by the Church, and the Fathers agree on few; many questions remain open to Catholic scholars.

  • Second Vatican Council, Dei Verbum (1965), §12

    Read Scripture for what its writers meant, with attention to literary forms, within the whole of Scripture, the living tradition and the analogy of faith.
