Galatians

6 chapters

At a glance

Author

Paul, who closes the letter in large letters in his own hand (6:11). The 1913 article notes that his authorship has never been seriously questioned.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistle to the Galatians”, Importance of the Epistle; Contents↗

Date

Uncertain, with three views in the 1913 article: before the Council of Jerusalem, about 49-50; about 53-54, held by most who favor the South Galatian theory; or as late as 57-58, by those who favor the North Galatian theory. Haydock, after Calmet, prefers Ephesus about 55, and Challoner gives about twenty-three years after the Ascension.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistle to the Galatians”, Date of the Epistle↗Haydock (1859)↗Challoner↗

Setting

Challoner and Haydock place it at Ephesus. Haydock rejects the Greek note that it was written from Rome, saying the 'marks' of 6:17 need not mean chains.

About this book

Audience

'The churches of Galatia' (1:2), mostly Gentile converts. The 1913 article sets out two views: Galatia proper, the Celtic north (Lightfoot), or the south of the Roman province, Pisidian Antioch, Iconium, Lystra and Derbe, visited on the first journey (Ramsay, Cornely); it favors the south.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistle to the Galatians”, The North and the South Galatian Theories↗

Purpose

To answer Judaizers who taught that Gentile Christians must be circumcised and keep the Law of Moses to be complete Christians, and who said Paul's apostleship came from men. Paul defends his call from Christ and shows that redemption comes through Christ, not the Law.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistle to the Galatians”, Why Written↗Challoner↗Haydock (1859)↗

Key themes

  • Paul's apostleship from Christ, in harmony with Peter, James and John (1-2)
  • Justification not by the works of the Law but by faith in Christ (2:16-3:14)
  • Abraham, the promise and the children of the free woman (3-4)
  • Christian freedom that is not license (5:1, 13)
  • Faith that works by charity (5:6)
  • The works of the flesh and the fruits of the Spirit (5:19-23)

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistle to the Galatians”, Contents of the Epistle; Importance↗

Structure

Three pairs of chapters: Paul's apostleship and his gospel's agreement with the great apostles (1-2); the powerlessness of circumcision and the Law, and redemption through Christ alone (3-4); and exhortations to use freedom well, with an epilogue in Paul's own hand (5-6).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistle to the Galatians”, Contents of the Epistle↗

How the Church reads it

The 1913 article reads the letter as Catholic teaching does: faith is a free gift of God, but the faith that avails is 'faith that worketh by charity' (5:6), in harmony with James. The Catechism draws on chapter 4 for the New Law as a law of freedom (CCC 1972), on 4:4-5 for the fullness of time and our adoption (CCC 422), on 3:27 for the baptized having put on Christ (CCC 1243), and on the Vulgate's list in 5:22-23 for the twelve fruits of the Holy Spirit (CCC 1832).

In the canon

Protocanonical; its authenticity and place in the canon have never been in doubt.

The old texts and the sources

Haydock's preface (1859 text)
The Galatians, soon after S. Paul had preached the gospel to them, were seduced by some false teachers, who had been Jews, and who were for obliging all Christians, even those who had been Gentiles, to observe circumcision, and the other ceremonies of the Mosaical law. In this epistle he refutes the pernicious doctrine of those teachers, and also their calumny against his mission and apostleship. The subject matter of this epistle is much the same as of that to the Romans. It was written at Ephesus, about twenty-three years after our Lord's ascension. (Challoner) — The Galatians were originally Gauls, who under their leader, Baennus, spread themselves over Greece, and at length passed over into Asia Minor, where they settled between Cappadocia and Phrygia, in the province afterwards called from them Galatia. It seems that S. Peter preached first in those parts; but it was only to the Jews, as may be gathered from the inscription of his first epistle, which he addresses to the Jews of Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia. But S. Paul was the first that preached to the Gentile inhabitants of this province. When he first preached to them, he was received as an angel from heaven, or rather, as Christ himself: he visited them oftener than once, and the Church he there formed was very considerable. It was the Jewish converts of Galatia (who, as we have before mentioned, were the spiritual children of S. Peter) that caused those troubles which gave rise to this epistle. They strongly advocated the legal observances; and making a handle of the high pre-eminence of S. Peter, they decried S. Paul, even calling in question his apostleship. They taught the necessity of circumcision, and other Mosaic rites, which the apostles then in part retained. Thus divisions were raised in this infant Church. On these accounts the apostle warmly asserts his apostleship, as being called by Christ himself. He shews that his doctrine was that of the other apostles, who, in the council of Jerusalem, four years before, testified their exemption from the legal observances. He teaches, that it is not by the law, but by faith, that the blessings of salvation are imparted to them. After establishing these more important parts of the epistle, he gives them instruction on various heads. The Greek subscription to this epistle informs us, that it was written from Rome. S. Jerom says, he wrote it when in chains. Theodoret says, it was the first epistle that S. Paul wrote from Rome. This opinion has probably been adopted from a mistaken interpretation of the text: I bear the marks of the wounds of Christ in my body. By these marks they understand chains, whilst the text equally applies to the mortifications and self-denials of a Christian. The contrary opinion is, that this epistle was written from Ephesus in the year of Christ 55. This is the more probable opinion, and is maintained by S. Greg. the Great, Ludovicus, Capellanus, Estius, Usher, Pearson, and many others. The authority of the Greek copies, in assigning the places whence the letters were written, has been long rejected by the learned. We find not such information in the more ancient Greek manuscripts of S. Germanus and Clermont, &c. (Calmet)
