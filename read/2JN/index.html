2 John

1 chapter

At a glance

Author

'The ancient' or 'the elder' (verse 1), whom tradition identifies with John the apostle. Polycarp seems to echo verse 7, Irenaeus quotes verse 10 as the words of John the Lord's disciple, and Haydock says the style shows it is the evangelist's. The 1913 article holds that 'the elder' means the apostle and rejects a separate 'John the Elder'.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles of Saint John”, Second Epistle↗Haydock (1859)↗

Date

Uncertain; generally supposed to come from the end of John's long life (1913 article, on the two short letters).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles of Saint John”, Third Epistle (closing remark on both short letters)↗Haydock (1859)↗

Setting

Probably Ephesus, while John governed the churches of Asia (Haydock; 1913 article).

About this book

Audience

'The lady Elect and her children' (verse 1). Challoner takes her for a Christian woman named Electa and her family; Jerome and the 1913 article take her for a particular church, which fits the closing greeting from 'thy sister Elect' (verse 13).

Challoner↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles of Saint John”, Second Epistle↗

Purpose

To praise the faithfulness of the elect lady's children, urge mutual love shown in keeping the commandments, and warn against deceivers who deny that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh; such teachers are not to be welcomed (verses 7-11).

Challoner↗Haydock (1859)↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles of Saint John”, Second Epistle↗

Key themes

  • Walking in truth (verse 4)
  • Love as keeping the commandment received from the beginning (verses 5-6)
  • Deceivers who do not confess Christ come in the flesh (verse 7)
  • Abiding in the teaching of Christ, and no welcome for false teachers (verses 9-11)
  • The hope of meeting face to face (verse 12)

Haydock (1859)↗Challoner↗

Structure

Two parts, following Haydock: a greeting and exhortation to charity (verses 1-6), and a warning against the deceivers with instructions on how to treat them (verses 7-13).

Haydock (1859)↗

How the Church reads it

The Catechism cites verse 7 with 1 John 4:2-3 for the Church's insistence, from apostolic times, on the true Incarnation of God's Son against the early denials of his flesh (CCC 465), and for the deception that marks the Church's final trial (CCC 675). Challoner draws from it a lesson for all who are in danger of being led astray.

CCC 465↗CCC 675↗Challoner↗

In the canon

One of the seven New Testament books once disputed: Eusebius counted the two short letters of John among the contested books and the Peshitto lacked them. They were received in the West after the fourth century, and the 1913 article says only Trent put their canonicity beyond dispute.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles of Saint John”, Second Epistle↗Haydock (1859)↗Council of Trent, Session IV↗

The old texts and the sources

Haydock's preface (1859 text)
This Second and Third Epistles of S. John were not at first received as canonical by all Churches. See Euseb. and S. Jerom, in Catalogo, and Tillemont, art. 9, on S. John, p. 349. The style sufficiently shews they were written by S. John the evangelist. The time and place whence they were written is uncertain. The design in both is to exhort those he writes to, to stand firm in the faith of Christ against all seducing heretics, to love one another, &c. (Witham) — But though these last two Epistles of the beloved apostle were frequently contested till the fourth or fifth century, from that period they have been regularly received; and even during the first ages, they were frequently cited by many of the holy Fathers as Catholic Scripture. This short Epistle may still be divided into two parts. The first, a congratulation to the lady he styles Elect, in which he felicitates her on account of the faith and virtue he had discovered in some of her children. He exhorts both them and their mother to be more and more confirmed in charity, as the essential and fundamental precept of Christianity, and of which the unequivocal mark is the observance of the other commandments. This part is from the beginning to v. 6, inclusive. The second, which begins with v. 7, and continues to the end, is to put them on their guard against the errors of Simon, of Cerinthus, of the Gnostics, and of Basilides, and prescribes the manner how they are to behave themselves towards these seducers. Though the time and place be uncertain, there is some appearance that it was written from Ephesus, when the apostle governed this Church and all the others of Asia.
Challoner's introduction (1749–52)
The Apostle commends Electa and her family for their steadfastness in the true faith and exhorts them to persevere, lest they lose the reward of their labours. He exhorts them to love one another. But with heretics to have no society, even not to salute them. Although this Epistle is written to a particular person, yet its instructions may serve as a lesson to others, especially to those who, from their connections, situation, or condition in life, are in danger of perversion.
