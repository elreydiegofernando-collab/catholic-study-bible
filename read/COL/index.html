Colossians

4 chapters

At a glance

Author

Paul, with Timothy (1:1), writing from prison. The 1913 article notes that the letter was used early and widely as Paul's and that no one doubted it until 1838.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistle to the Colossians”, Authenticity of the Epistle; Objections↗Challoner↗

Date

During Paul's first imprisonment at Rome, A.D. 61-63, sent at the same time as Ephesians and Philemon (1913 article). Challoner says it was written in prison about the same time as Ephesians and Philippians.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistle to the Colossians”, opening paragraph↗Challoner↗

Setting

Rome, where Paul lived under guard in his own lodging (Acts 28:16-30). Tychicus carried the letter, with Onesimus, the runaway slave of Philemon (4:7-9).

About this book

Audience

The church at Colossae in Phrygia, near Laodicea and Hierapolis, founded by Epaphras and never visited by Paul (2:1). Its members were mostly Gentile converts.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistle to the Colossians”, Readers Addressed↗Challoner↗Haydock (1859)↗

Purpose

To warn against false teachers, reported to Paul by Epaphras, who pressed Jewish observances such as Sabbaths, new moons and food rules, and a worship of angels under a show of humility and 'philosophy' (2:8, 16-23). Paul answers by setting out Christ's supremacy over all creation and all powers.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistle to the Colossians”, Why Written↗Haydock (1859)↗

Key themes

  • Christ the image of the invisible God, firstborn of all creation, in whom all things were made (1:15-17)
  • Christ the head of the body, the Church, reconciling all things by the blood of his cross (1:18-20)
  • The fullness of the Godhead dwelling bodily in Christ (2:9)
  • Freedom from human traditions and the shadows of the Law (2:8, 16-17)
  • Risen with Christ, seek the things above; put on the new man (3:1-17)
  • Household duties (3:18-4:1)

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistle to the Colossians”, Contents↗

Structure

A doctrinal and polemical part on Christ's dignity and the errors at Colossae (1-2), and a practical part on the new life, household duties, prayer and greetings (3-4).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistle to the Colossians”, Contents↗

How the Church reads it

The Catechism draws on 1:16-17 for creation through the Son and for the angels as created through and for him (CCC 291, 331), on 1:18 for Christ as head of the Church (CCC 792), on 1:24 for uniting one's sufferings with Christ's for his body (CCC 618, 1508), and on 1:15-20 as one of the early Church's hymns (CCC 2641). The 1913 article adds that 2:18 condemns a worship of angels that put them above Christ, not the Catholic invocation of angels, and that 'the tradition of men' (2:8) is no slight on the apostolic traditions Paul elsewhere commands (2 Thessalonians 2:14 Douay = 2:15).

In the canon

Protocanonical; it was in the earliest collections of Paul's letters. The 'epistle of the Laodiceans' mentioned at 4:16 has not survived as a separate letter.

The old texts and the sources

Haydock's preface (1859 text)
Colosse was a city of Phrygia, near Laodicea. It does not appear that S. Paul had preached there himself, (see C. ii. 1.) but that the Colossians were converted by Epaphras, a disciple of the apostles. However, as S. Paul was the great apostle of the Gentiles, he wrote this epistle to the Colossians when he was in prison, and about the same time that he wrote to the Ephesians and Philippians. The exhortations and doctrine it contains, are similar to those which are set forth in his epistle to the Ephesians. S. Chrys. takes notice, that the epistles he wrote in prison seem even more spiritual than the rest: the chief design of which was to hinder them from being seduced by false teachers. (Challoner; Witham) — The Colossians were first instructed in the faith by Epaphras, who is considered their first bishop. He was a prisoner, at Rome, with S. Paul, when this epistle was written. The intent of it was to disabuse the Colossians of worshipping the Angels; for Cerinthus and others, had taught them to look upon Angels as superior to Christ, whom they looked upon as a mere man; to observe the law of Moses, with all its legal rites and ceremonies. He begins his epistle by insisting chiefly on the exalted state of Christ, saying that he is the image of the invisible God, the first-born of every creature, by whom all things visible and invisible were created, whether thrones, principalities, or powers, and that in him the divinity essentially exists. From this he proves the inutility of the ceremonies of the law, &c. (Fleury and Calmet) and takes great pains to prevent their relapsing either into paganism or Judaism. (Bible de Vence)
