Ecclesiastes

At a glance

Author

Challoner and Haydock follow tradition in naming Solomon, the 'preacher' (Hebrew Qoheleth), and the 1913 article defends that tradition. It reports, however, that most modern commentators judge the style to point to a later writer, and Haydock says that Solomon's writing it after his repentance, though generally supposed, is very uncertain.

Challoner, Introduction↗Haydock (1859), Preface↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Ecclesiastes”, The Author of the Book↗

Date

If Solomon wrote it, it belongs near the end of his life, and the 1913 article explains its somber tone by the judgments that then fell on him. Critics who deny his authorship, it reports, have proposed every date from Solomon to 200 B.C., and Graetz the reign of Herod.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Ecclesiastes”, The Author of the Book↗

Setting

Jerusalem, in the voice of the king who has tried wisdom, pleasure, wealth and work 'under the sun' and found them vanity.

About this book

Audience

The 1913 article says Qoheleth meant his teaching not for one man or one people but for mankind, arguing from reason rather than from revelation.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Ecclesiastes”, The Author of the Book↗

Purpose

Challoner says Solomon sets forth the vanity of this world's goods to draw men's hearts away from such empty toys. The 1913 article sums up its lesson: true happiness is not found on earth, so fear God and keep his commandments, which is the whole of man (12:13).

Challoner, Introduction↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Ecclesiastes”↗

Key themes

  • 'Vanity of vanities': the emptiness of earthly goods sought for themselves.
  • The limits of human wisdom.
  • Enjoying God's gifts with moderation and in his fear.
  • Death, and remembering one's Creator in youth (12:1-7).
  • Fear God and keep his commandments (12:13).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Ecclesiastes”↗Challoner, Introduction↗CCC 1007↗

Structure

The 1913 article finds an introduction (everything human is vain, 1:1-11), three parts on vanity in private life, in civil life and in the search for wisdom, and an epilogue that returns to the fear of God.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Ecclesiastes”↗

How the Church reads it

The 1913 article notes that the Fathers drew on it for edification and consolation, St. Gregory Thaumaturgus in a paraphrase, St. Gregory of Nyssa in eight homilies, and Hugh of St. Victor in nineteen. The Catechism uses its call to remember the Creator before death (12:1, 7) in teaching on death as the end of earthly life (CCC 1007). Haydock, citing Worthington, says it refutes worldly notions of happiness and shows that happiness lies in the service and enjoyment of God.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Ecclesiastes”, The Author of the Book↗CCC 1007↗Haydock (1859), Preface↗

In the canon

A protocanonical book (Trent, Session IV; CCC 120). The 1913 article notes that some Jews objected to keeping it in the canon until Hillel's school settled the matter, and Haydock that St. Jerome reports early hesitation; the article adds that its inspiration was reaffirmed against Theodore of Mopsuestia at the Fifth Ecumenical Council (553).

The old texts and the sources

Haydock's preface (1859 text)
This Book is called Ecclesiastes, or the preacher, (in Hebrew, Coheleth) because in it Solomon, as an excellent preacher, setteth forth the vanity of the things of this world, to withdraw the hearts and affections of men from such empty toys. (Challoner) — Coheleth is a feminine noun, to indicate the elegance of the discourse. It is very difficult to discriminate the objections of free-thinkers from the real sentiments of the author. It is most generally supposed that Solomon wrote this after his repentance; but this is very uncertain. S. Jerom (in C. xii. 12.) informs us that the collectors of the sacred books had some scruple about admitting this; and Luther speaks of it with great disrespect: (Coll. conviv.) but the Church has always maintained its authority. See Conc. v. Act. 4. Philast. 132. (Calmet) — It refutes the false notions of worldlings, concerning felicity; and shews that it consists in the service of God and fruition. (Worthington)
Challoner's introduction (1749–52)
This Book is called Ecclesiastes, or The Preacher, (in Hebrew, Coheleth,) because in it, Solomon, as an excellent preacher, setteth forth the vanity of the things of this world: to withdraw the hearts and affections of men from such empty toys.
