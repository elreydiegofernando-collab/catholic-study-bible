2 Peter

3 chapters

At a glance

Author

The letter names Simon Peter, a witness of the Transfiguration, writing his 'second epistle' as death approaches (1:1, 14, 16-18; 3:1). Its authorship was doubted in the early centuries, and Jerome explained the change of style by Peter's use of different interpreters. The 1913 article judges Peter's authorship solidly probable but hard to prove with certainty.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles of Saint Peter”, Second Epistle: A. Authenticity↗Haydock (1859)↗Challoner↗

Date

Shortly before Peter's martyrdom. The 1913 article places it after 1 Peter (63-64) and before his death (64-65); Witham, in Haydock, gives about 66, and Challoner about thirty-five years after the Ascension.

Setting

About this book

Audience

Believed to be the same Christians of Asia Minor as the first letter, mostly converted Gentiles (1913 article).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles of Saint Peter”, Second Epistle: B. Recipients↗

Purpose

Peter's farewell: to urge the faithful to add every virtue to their faith and to warn them against false teachers of corrupt life who denied the Lord's second coming. Haydock calls it the apostle's spiritual testament.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles of Saint Peter”, Second Epistle: B. Occasion and Object↗Haydock (1859)↗Challoner↗

Key themes

  • Made partakers of the divine nature (1:4)
  • Faith growing into virtue, knowledge and charity (1:5-11)
  • The Transfiguration and the sure word of prophecy, not of private interpretation (1:16-21)
  • False teachers and God's judgments (2)
  • The day of the Lord: the world dissolved by fire, new heavens and a new earth (3:3-13)
  • Paul's letters, with some things hard to understand that the unlearned wrest (3:15-16)

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles of Saint Peter”, Second Epistle: D. Analysis↗Challoner↗

Structure

An opening on Christ's gifts and the call to virtue as Peter's death nears (1:1-15); the body: Christ's coming proved by the Transfiguration and the prophets, the false teachers condemned, and their denial of the end refuted (1:16-3:13); a moral conclusion recalling Paul's letters (3:14-17); and a doxology (3:18). The 1913 article reports Ladeuze's view that two sections were transposed by an early copyist.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles of Saint Peter”, Second Epistle: D. Analysis; A.(2)↗

How the Church reads it

The Catechism takes 1:4, 'partakers of the divine nature', as a summary of why the Word became flesh (CCC 460) and 3:13 for the new heavens and new earth (CCC 1043). Vatican II and the Catechism cite 1:19-21 and 3:15-16 for the inspiration of Scripture and the Church's faith in it (Dei Verbum 11; CCC 105). Haydock notes what faith teaches about the end, that the world will end, by fire, and be changed, and treats the details as open.

In the canon

One of the seven New Testament books once disputed. Eusebius counted it among the contested books though he accepted it himself; Haydock notes it was acknowledged as canonical by the end of the fourth century, and the Roman synod of 382 under Damasus ascribed it to Peter (1913 article).

The old texts and the sources

Haydock's preface (1859 text)
This Epistle, though not at first received as canonical, was acknowledged as such about the end of the fourth age. See Euseb. l. iii. Hist. c. 3. S. Jer. de Vir. Illust. Tillem. art. 33. The design, as it appears, C. i. 13. and C. iii. 1. was to give them admonitions and instructions against teachers of false doctrine, particularly against the Simonites. It seems to have been written a little before his martyrdom, about the year 66. (Witham) — In this epistle S. Peter says, (C. iii.) "Behold this is the second epistle I write unto you:" and before, (C. i. 14.) "Being assured that the putting off of this my tabernacle is at hand." This shews that it was written a very short time before his martyrdom, which was about thirty-five years after our Lord's ascension. In this epistle he admonishes the faithful to be mindful of the great gifts they received from God, and to join all other virtues with their faith. He warns them against false teachers, by describing their practices and foretelling their punishments. He describes the dissolution of this world by fire, and the day of judgment. (Challoner) — This epistle may be considered as the spiritual testament or last will of the apostle, as it contains his last admonitions to the faithful. He first calls their attention to the care they should have of their sanctification and perfection, next to the perils that concern the Church on the part of heretics, those that menaced her in her infant state, and those that will assail her in the latter days, which includes an invincible proof of her perpetuity; for it is the same infallible and indefectible Church that is to encounter the latter as the former trials, but always with promised success. Hence the great S. John Chrysostom says: the same day that shall see the Church of God ended, shall see the end of the world; and to these continued struggles shall succeed perfect peace, to be enjoyed through a blissful eternity. If some are still found to object, that the present epistle was not written by S. Peter, on account of the marked difference of the style, S. Jerom removes this objection thus: S. Peter employed different interpreters, sometimes Glaucias, and sometimes S. Mark; hence the difference of the style, from the diversity of his scribes. S. Mark was with him when he penned the first, but was not with him when he dictated the present. The present epistle contains, as we said above, an account of the last dreadful trials that are to assail the faithful before the end of time; but all that faith teaches us on that subject is: first, That the world will have an end; secondly, that it will end by fire; and thirdly, that the world will not be destroyed, but changed and perfected. Hence all that is said with regard to the duration of the world; on the nature and quality of the fire that is to burn and purify the world; if it be to precede or follow the last judgment, all is problematical, all is doubtful. Hence the Christian knows a good deal, who knows how to entertain proper doubts.
