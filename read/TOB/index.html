Tobit†

Douay: Tobias · 14 chapters · deuterocanonical

At a glance

Author

The 1913 article thinks the elder Tobias probably wrote the first-person part (1:1-3:6 in the Greek and most texts) and that father and son wrote all but the last chapter, as Raphael bade them record what God had done (12:20). Haydock thinks both men left records from which the book was compiled with a few additions.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Tobias”, II. Book of Tobias, G. Origin↗Haydock (1859), Preface↗

Date

If the story is taken as history, the 1913 article reasons, it was first written during the exile in the 7th century B.C.; Haydock says during or after the Babylonian captivity. The article reports that almost all Protestant scholars put it after the Exile, Ewald about 350 B.C. and others later still.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Tobias”, II. Book of Tobias, G. Origin↗Haydock (1859), Preface↗

Setting

Ninive, among the exiles of the tribe of Nephthali under the Assyrian kings, with the young Tobias's journey through Ecbatana to Rages in Media. Haydock notes the family records seem to have been kept at Ecbatana, which would explain why the story was little known among other Jews.

About this book

Audience

The 1913 article holds that the book was meant for all Jews, not only those of the Dispersion: a lesson in fidelity for God's people far from home.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Tobias”, II. Book of Tobias, D. Purpose↗

Purpose

The 1913 article says the chief purpose is evidently to show that God is faithful to those who are faithful to him, with almsgiving the most prominent of several secondary lessons. Challoner praises its excellent lessons of piety, patience and perfect resignation to God's will.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Tobias”, II. Book of Tobias, D. Purpose↗Challoner, Introduction↗

Key themes

  • God's faithfulness to the faithful, even in exile and suffering.
  • Almsgiving, burial of the dead and the works of mercy.
  • The angel Raphael as guide, healer and the one who presents prayers to God.
  • Marriage as a holy vocation begun in prayer.
  • Prayer, fasting and almsgiving together (12:8).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Tobias”, II. Book of Tobias, D. Purpose↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Tobias”, II. Book of Tobias, C. Contents↗CCC 336↗CCC 2361↗CCC 1434↗

Structure

The 1913 article, using the Vulgate, finds two parts: the fidelity of the elder Tobias and of Sara to the Lord (1:1-3:25), and the Lord's fidelity to them through the angel Raphael (4:1-12:22), who guides the young Tobias, frees Sara from the demon, and cures the elder Tobias's blindness. A hymn of thanksgiving and the later history of father and son conclude the book (13-14).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Tobias”, II. Book of Tobias, C. Contents↗Haydock (1859), Preface↗

How the Church reads it

The Catechism quotes the prayer of Tobias and Sara on their wedding night as a witness to the dignity of married love (CCC 2361), and says Ruth and Tobit bear moving witness to a high sense of marriage (CCC 1611). It draws on the book for the watchful care of the angels (CCC 336) and for prayer, fasting and almsgiving as forms of penance (CCC 1434). The 1913 article shows that the Fathers from St. Polycarp onward cited it as Scripture, and Haydock quotes St. Augustine that holy Tobias is given us as an example of how to live what we read.

In the canon

A deuterocanonical book: the 1913 article explains that it was in the Alexandrian (Septuagint) canon but not the Palestinian one. The Church always held it inspired; it is in the earliest Christian canon lists (Hippo 393, Carthage 397 and 419, Innocent I 405), and Trent's Session IV settled its canonicity for Catholics (CCC 120 lists it).

Historical notes

Historical note23 June 1905 · Pontifical Biblical Commission

On narratives only apparently historical (De narrationibus specietenus tantum historicis)

ASS 38 (1905-06) 124

The Commission answered that a book generally held to be historical may not be taken as wholly or partly not history in the proper sense unless solid arguments prove that the sacred writer did not intend to write history. The 1913 article cites it to support the historicity of Tobias, judging the arguments against it mere conjectures.

The Pontifical Biblical Commission's answers of 1905-1915 bound Catholics when they were issued (Pius X, Praestantia Scripturae, 1907). In 1955 the Commission's secretary and under-secretary wrote, in an unofficial statement, that scholars are free on such points except where they touch faith and morals. Since 1971 the Commission has been an advisory body. These notes record what was decided and when; they are not badges.

The old texts and the sources

Haydock's preface (1859 text)
This Book takes its name from the holy man Tobias, whose wonderful virtues are herein recorded. It contains most excellent documents of great piety, extraordinary patience, and of perfect resignation to the will of God. His humble prayer was heard, and the angel Raphael was sent to relieve him: he is thankful, and praises the Lord, calling on the children of Israel to do the same. Having lived to the age of one hundred and two years, he exhorts his son and grandsons to piety, foretells the destruction of Ninive, and the rebuilding of Jerusalem: he dies happily. (Challoner) — The Jews themselves have a great regard for the book of Tobias; (Grot. Sixtus Senens. viii.) which Origen (ad Afric.) says they "read in Hebrew," meaning probably the Chaldee, (Calmet) out of which language S. Jerom translated it, preferring to displease the Pharisaical Jews, rather than not to satisfy the desires of the holy bishops Chromatius and Heliodorus. Ep. t. iii. (Worthington) — The Greek version seems to have been taken from another copy, or it has been executed with greater liberty by the Hellenist Jews, between the times of the Septuagint and of Theodotion. (Calmet) — Huet and Prideaux esteem it more original; and Houbigant has translated it in his Bible, as the Council of Trent only spoke of the Latin editions then extant; and S. Jerom followed in his version the Hebrew one of a Jew, as he did not understand the Chaldee. (Haydock) — The Syriac and the modern Hebrew edition of Fagius, agree mostly with the Greek, as that of Munster and another Hebrew copy of Huet, and the Arabic version, both unpublished, are more conformable to the Vulgate. The most ancient Latin version used before S. Jerom, was taken from the Greek; and the Fathers who lived in those ages, speak of it when they call the book of Tobias canonical. S. Aug. leaves it, however, to adopt S. Jerom's version, in his Mirrour. The copies of all these versions vary greatly, (Calmet) though the substance of the history is still the same; and in all we discover the virtues of a good parent, of a dutiful son, and virtuous husband, beautifully described. (Haydock) — "The servant of God, holy Tobias, is given to us after the law for an example, that we might know how to practise what we read; and that if temptations assail us, we may not depart from the fear of God, nor expect help from any other." S. Aug. q. 119. ex utroque Test. — The four first chapters exhibit the holy life of old Tobias, and the eight following, the journey and affairs of his son, directed by Raphael. In the two last chapters they praise God, and the elder Tobias foretells the better state of the commonwealth. (Worthington) — It is probable that both left records, from which this work has been compiled, with a few additional observations. It was written during (Calmet) or after the captivity of Babylon. (Estius) — The Jews had then little communication with each other, in different kingdoms. Tobias was not allowed to go into Media, under Sennacherib; and it is probable that the captives at Babylon would be under similar restrictions; so that we do not need to wonder that they were unacquainted with this history of a private family, the records of which seem to have been kept at Ecbatana. The original Chaldee is entirely lost, so that it is impossible to ascertain whether the Greek or the Vulg. be more conformable to it. The chronology of the latter seems however more accurate, as the elder Tobias foretold the destruction of Ninive, twenty-three years before the event, which his son just beheld verified, dying in the 18th year of king Josias. The accounts which appear to sectaries to be fabulous, may easily be explained. (Houbigant) — Josephus and Philo omit this history. (Calmet)
