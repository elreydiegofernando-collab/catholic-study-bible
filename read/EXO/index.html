Exodus

40 chapters

At a glance

Author

Tradition names Moses, and the book itself says that Moses wrote down 'all the words of the Lord' (24:4) and the words of the covenant (34:27), passages the 1913 article takes as direct witness to his writing. Challoner calls it 'the second book of Moses'.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Pentateuch”, III. Authenticity, A. Testimony of Sacred Scripture, (1) Witness of the Pentateuch↗Challoner, Introduction↗

Date

Tradition dates it to the lifetime of Moses: the 15th century B.C. on Usher's chronology, which Haydock follows, or the 13th century if Israel's captivity fell under Ramses II, as a scholar cited in the 1913 article held. The 1906 decree allowed later inspired additions; the critics the article answers put the final form after the Exile.

Ecclesiastical Decisions↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Pentateuch”, III. Authenticity, E. Opponents of the Mosaic Authorship, (2) Present Hypothesis of Documents↗

Setting

It opens in Egypt, where Israel has grown into a people and is enslaved by a new Pharao; Haydock's preface, citing Du Hamel, puts the slavery at about ninety years. It follows the departure through the Red Sea and the desert to Mount Sinai, where the covenant is made and the tabernacle set up. Challoner counts about 145 years from Joseph's death to the tabernacle; Haydock, citing Tirinus, gives 143.

About this book

Audience

Israel, the people freed from Egypt and bound to God at Sinai. The 1913 article notes that its laws are woven into the people's history, which is why the book is counted among the historical books.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Pentateuch”, II. Analysis, B. Exodus↗

Purpose

To tell how God freed his people, made them his own by covenant, gave them his Law, and came to dwell among them in the tabernacle. The 1913 article sums up the contrast with the next book: Exodus shows what God did for his people, Leviticus what the people must do for God.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Pentateuch”, II. Analysis, B. Exodus↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Pentateuch”, II. Analysis, C. Leviticus↗

Key themes

  • Liberation from slavery: the Passover and the crossing of the Red Sea.
  • God reveals his name to Moses at the burning bush.
  • The covenant at Sinai and the gift of the Ten Commandments.
  • God dwelling among his people in the tabernacle.
  • Moses the intercessor, pleading for the people after the golden calf.

CCC 205–209↗CCC 2056–2063↗CCC 2577↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Pentateuch”, II. Analysis, B. Exodus↗

Structure

The 1913 article finds a short introduction (1:1-7) and three parts. The first (1:8-13:16) covers the oppression, the call of Moses, the plagues and the Passover; the second (13:17-18:27), the journey from the Red Sea to Sinai; the third (19:1-40:38), the Sinai covenant and its laws, the golden calf and the covenant's renewal, and the making of the tabernacle.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Pentateuch”, II. Analysis, B. Exodus↗

How the Church reads it

The Church reads the Exodus as a figure of Christ's work: the Catechism says the Red Sea crossing announces the liberation of Baptism (CCC 1221), and it explains the Eucharist as a memorial in the biblical sense of the Passover, which makes the Exodus present to each generation (CCC 1363-1364). The Ten Commandments given at Sinai remain the core of the Church's moral teaching (CCC 2056-2063). Haydock, citing Augustine, adds that the laws, sacrifices and tabernacle were meant to prefigure the Christian order, and that Moses was a type of Christ.

In the canon

Exodus is the second of the 'five books of Moses' in Trent's list of the canonical Scriptures (Session IV), and it is in the Catechism's list of the canon (CCC 120).

Historical notes

Historical note27 June 1906 · Pontifical Biblical Commission

On the Mosaic authenticity of the Pentateuch (De mosaica authentia Pentateuchi)

ASS 39 (1906) 377

The Commission answered that the critics' arguments did not justify denying that Moses is the author of the Pentateuch. It allowed that he used written or oral sources, that he may have had others write the work under his direction and then approved it, and that the text later received inspired additions, updated wording and copyists' errors.

Historical note16 January 1948 · Pontifical Biblical Commission

Letter to Cardinal Suhard on the sources of the Pentateuch and the literary form of Genesis 1-11

AAS 40 (1948) 45-48

In a letter to the Archbishop of Paris, the Commission said scholars no longer doubt that the Pentateuch drew on sources and that its laws grew over time, and it invited Catholic scholars to study these questions freely. It added that Genesis 1-11 does not fit classical or modern categories of history-writing, yet relates in simple, figurative language the basic truths presupposed by salvation and a popular account of human origins.

Pontifical Biblical Commission (1948)↗

The Pontifical Biblical Commission's answers of 1905-1915 bound Catholics when they were issued (Pius X, Praestantia Scripturae, 1907). In 1955 the Commission's secretary and under-secretary wrote, in an unofficial statement, that scholars are free on such points except where they touch faith and morals. Since 1971 the Commission has been an advisory body. These notes record what was decided and when; they are not badges.

The old texts and the sources

Haydock's preface (1859 text)
The second Book of Moses is called Exodus from the Greek word Εξοδος, which signifies going out; because it contains the history of the going out of the children of Israel out of Egypt. The Hebrews, from the words with which it begins, call it Veelle Shemoth: These are the names. (Challoner) — It contains the space of 143 years, till the beginning of the second year after the liberation of the Israelites. (Tirinus) — Their slavery is described in the first chapters; and is supposed to have continued ninety years. (Du Hamel) — The laws prescribed by God to his people, the sacrifices, tabernacle, &c. were all intended to prefigure the Christian dispensation. S. Aug. de C. D. vii. 31. — Moses himself was a type of Jesus Christ, who was rejected by the synagogue, and received by the Gentiles, as the Jewish Legislator was abandoned by his mother, and educated by the Egyptian princess. She delivers him back to his mother; and thus the Jews will, at last, acknowledge our Saviour. (Du Hamel) — God deigns to address his people in the character of a powerful Eastern monarch, and requires the like attention. He appoints his ministers, like guards, to attend before his tabernacle, &c. The laws which he enacts, are such as suited the Jewish people: they were not to rise all at once to perfection; but these laws guide them, as it were, on the road. They are infinitely more perfect than those of the surrounding nations. (Calmet)
Challoner's introduction (1749–52)
The Second Book of Moses is called EXODUS, from the Greek word EXODOS, which signifies going out: because it contains the history of the going out of the children of Israel out of Egypt. The Hebrews, from the words with which it begins, call it VEELLE SEMOTH: These are the names. It contains transactions for 145 years; that is, from the death of Joseph to the erecting of the tabernacle.
