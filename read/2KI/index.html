2 Kings

Douay: 4 Kings · 25 chapters

At a glance

Author

Its authorship is that of 3 Kings, with which it forms one work: Jeremias (the Talmud, thought probable by the 1913 article), a succession of prophets (Challoner), or Esdras compiling after the captivity (Calmet, in Haydock). The 1913 article thinks the last verses (25:27-30) may have been added by a different hand.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Third and Fourth Books of Kings”↗Challoner, Introduction↗Haydock (1859), Preface↗

Date

Not finished before the middle of the Exile: the last event told is King Joachin's release from prison in 562 B.C.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Third and Fourth Books of Kings”↗

Setting

From Ochozias of Israel and the taking up of Elias to the fall of Samaria to Assyria and finally the destruction of Jerusalem by Babylon and the exile of Juda; Calmet, in Haydock, counts 308 years.

About this book

Audience

The exiles of Juda, who needed to understand why the kingdoms fell and whether God's promise to David still stood.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Third and Fourth Books of Kings”↗

Purpose

The 1913 article says the epilogue after the fall of Israel (17:7 and following) states the writer's aim: the people worshipped strange gods and rejected the prophets' warnings, and Juda followed the same path. Yet the book also recalls that God, just in his anger, was merciful for David's sake, so the remnant could keep hope in the Messias.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Third and Fourth Books of Kings”↗

Key themes

  • Eliseus receives the spirit of Elias and works wonders.
  • The fall of Samaria explained by idolatry and refusal to hear the prophets.
  • Reforming kings: Ezechias's trust in God, Josias and the book of the law.
  • The fall of Jerusalem and the Babylonian exile.
  • Hope kept alive: David's line survives in Joachin.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Third and Fourth Books of Kings”↗CCC 2684↗Haydock (1859), Preface↗

Structure

In the 1913 article's three-part plan for 3 and 4 Kings, this book holds the rest of the second part, the kingdoms side by side to the fall of Israel (1-17), and all of the third, Juda alone from Ezechias to the destruction of Jerusalem (18-25).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Third and Fourth Books of Kings”↗

How the Church reads it

The Catechism cites the handing on of the spirit of Elias to Eliseus as an image of how a saint's charism can be shared by followers (CCC 2684). Jesus himself points to Eliseus's healing of Naaman the Syrian as a sign of God's mercy to the Gentiles (Luke 4:27). Haydock notes that David's line held the throne about 480 years and, after the captivity, kept some honor until the coming of Christ.

CCC 2684↗Luke 4:27Haydock (1859), Preface↗

In the canon

This is the fourth of Trent's 'four books of Kings' (Session IV); the Catechism lists it as 2 Kings (CCC 120). It was one book with 3 Kings in Hebrew.

Council of Trent, Session IV, p. 18↗CCC 120↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Third and Fourth Books of Kings”↗

The old texts and the sources

Haydock's preface (1859 text)
This Book brings us to the conclusion of the kingdom of Israel, (C. xvii.) and to the captivity of Juda, at Babylon. C. xxv. We behold some virtuous princes reigning over the two tribes, while the ten were uniformly governed by men of perverse morals. (Worthington) — Yet there were some who adhered to the Lord in both kingdoms. David and his descendants (Haydock) occupy the throne near 480 years; and, after the captivity, continue in some degree of honour till the coming of Christ. (Worthington) — But various families rule over Israel; some by usurpation, (Haydock) others by God's appointment: who thus chooses to chastise the wicked. He still watches over his Church, and sends his prophets for the instruction of all. (Worthington) — We have enquired in the preface of the former Book, who composed this. (Haydock) — The kingdom of Israel subsists about 250, (Worthington) or 254 years. This Book contains the transactions of 308 years. (Calmet) — But the chronology is extremely perplexed. To the sixth year of Ezechias, when Israel was led away captive, the kingdom of Juda seems to have lasted 260, and that of Israel only 241 years, though they both commenced at the same period. The errors regard the kings of Isreal, according to Houbigant, who would assign the following years to the respective kings of Juda and Israel. 1. Of Juda: Solomon, 40; Roboam, 17; Abiam, 3; Asa, 41, Josaphat, 25; Joram, 8; Ochozias, 1; (the same is said to have begun to reign in the preceding year, the 11th of Joram, 4 K. ix. 29, incorrectly) Athalia, 6; Joas, 40; Amasias, 29; (he reigns 15 after the death of Joas, king of Isreal) Azarias, 52; Joatham, 16; Achaz, 16; Ezechias, 6; in which year, the three hundredth from the commencement of Solomon's reign, and the two hundred and sixtieth of the kingdom of Juda, Samaria was taken. 2. The kings of Israel: Jeroboam, 22; Nadab, 2; Baasa, 24; Ela, 2; Zambri, 7 days; Amri, 12; Achab, 22; Ochozias, 2; Joram, 12; Jehu, 28; Joachaz, 17; Joas, 16; Jeroboam, 41; Zacharias, 10½; (in the text 10 is omitted. H.) Sellum, 1 month; Manahem, 10; Phaceia, 2; Phacee, 30; (in the text, 20.; Haydock) Osee, 9; in all, 261½ years, (Houbig. Chron. Sac.) or 261 years and 7 months. The variation of 19 months, which still appears, may be owing to some of the years being incomplete. (Haydock) — 3. After a reign of 28 years over Juda, Ezechias is succeeded by Manasses, who reigns 55: Amon, 2; Josias, 31; Joachaz, a few months; Eliacim, or Joakim, 11; Joachin, Conias, or Jechonias, had reigned ten years with his father. After three months and ten days reigning alone, he is led away to Babylon with part of the people. Matthanias, or Sedecias, is appointed in his stead; but proving refractory, is also, after nine years, deprived of his sight, and conducted with 832 of his subjects to Babylon. Nabuchodonosor had already led away 3023, under Joachin. After the death of Godolias, who was left to govern the miserable remains of the people, A. 3417, he made 745 more captives, and thus an end was put to the kingdom of Juda. The scourge had been retarded for some time, by the repentance of Manasses, and the prayers of the prophets. (Calmet)
