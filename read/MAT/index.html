Matthew

28 chapters

At a glance

Author

Tradition names the apostle Matthew, the tax collector also called Levi. Papias, Irenaeus, Origen, Eusebius and Jerome report that he wrote first, in the language of the Jews of Palestine; that original is lost, and the Church has always used the Greek Gospel as his. The 1913 article judged Aramaic the more likely original language and noted that no one knows who made the Greek version.

Challoner↗Haydock (1859)↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Gospel of St. Matthew”, II.A Testimony of Tradition↗

Date

Haydock dates it about A.D. 41 and Challoner about six years after the Ascension. The 1913 article reports that most Catholic scholars of its day favored 40-45 and some 60-67, and it argues from 24:15-16 that it was written before Jerusalem fell in 70. It also lists critics who put it after 70.

Haydock (1859)↗Challoner↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Gospel of St. Matthew”, VI. Date and Place of Composition↗

Setting

Palestine, before Matthew left to preach in other lands. Eusebius says he wrote as he was leaving, and Jerome that he published it in Judea; Haydock gives the same account.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Gospel of St. Matthew”, IV. Object; V. Destination↗Haydock (1859)↗

About this book

Audience

Jewish Christians, as Papias, Irenaeus, Origen, Eusebius and Jerome agree. The book takes Jewish customs and Palestinian places for granted, and Zahn, quoted in the 1913 article, thought it was also meant for Jews not yet converted.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Gospel of St. Matthew”, V. Destination of the Gospel↗

Purpose

To show from his words and deeds that Jesus is the Messiah the prophets foretold and the founder of the Kingdom of God. It also shows that Israel's leaders shut themselves out by unbelief, and that the Gospel is to go to all nations.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Gospel of St. Matthew”, IV. Object and Doctrinal Teaching↗

Key themes

  • Jesus the Messiah and Son of David, fulfilling the prophets (1:22-23; 2:6, 15)
  • The Kingdom of heaven and its charter, the Sermon on the Mount (5-7)
  • Peter's confession and the promise of the Church and the keys (16:16-19)
  • The rejection of the Messiah by Israel's leaders (21-27)
  • The mission to all nations and baptism in the name of the Father, Son and Holy Spirit (28:18-20)

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Gospel of St. Matthew”, III. Plan; IV. Object↗

Structure

Infancy (1-2); public ministry in Galilee (3-18), on the way to Jerusalem (19-20) and in Jerusalem (21-25); Passion and Resurrection (26-28). Five long discourses (5-7, 10, 13, 18, 24-25) end with the same formula, and the evangelist likes groups of three, five and seven.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Gospel of St. Matthew”, III. Plan and Contents↗

How the Church reads it

The Church reads 16:16-19 as Christ's promise of a real primacy to Peter (Vatican I, Pastor Aeternus, ch. I), and the Catechism explains the rock and the keys as Peter's office (CCC 552-553, 881). The closing command grounds the baptismal formula and the mission (28:19-20; CCC 232, 1223), the Beatitudes and the Sermon on the Mount are read as the heart of the New Law (CCC 1716, 1965-1968), and Trent cites the words of institution (26:26-28) for the Real Presence (Session XIII, ch. I). Vatican II affirms the apostolic origin and the history of all four Gospels (Dei Verbum 18-19).

In the canon

Protocanonical and never disputed. It stands first among the Gospels in Trent's list and, the 1913 article notes, heads the New Testament in the early canons of Laodicea and Athanasius.

Historical notes

Historical note19 June 1911 · Pontifical Biblical Commission

Quaestiones de evangelio secundum Matthaeum (On the Gospel according to Matthew)

AAS 3 (1911) 294-296

Held that the apostle Matthew is the author, that he wrote first and in the language of the Jews of Palestine, and that he finished before Jerusalem fell and before Paul came to Rome. It held the Greek text to be the same in substance and defended the history of the infancy chapters and of passages on Peter's primacy (16:17-19), the baptismal command (28:19-20) and the apostles' confession (14:33).

Pontifical Biblical Commission (1911)↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Gospel of Saint Matthew (Biblical Commission)”↗

Historical note26 June 1912 · Pontifical Biblical Commission

De quaestione synoptica (On the Synoptic question)

AAS 4 (1912) 465

Allowed scholars to dispute freely how the first three Gospels are related, by oral or written tradition or by dependence of one on another, within the earlier decisions. It held that the two-source theory could not be maintained.

Pontifical Biblical Commission (1912)↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Synoptics (Biblical Commission)”↗

Historical note1 July 1933 · Pontifical Biblical Commission

De falsa duorum textuum biblicorum interpretatione (On the false interpretation of two biblical texts)

AAS 25 (1933) 344

Answered that 16:26 ('what doth it profit a man...') may not be read in its literal sense as speaking only of this earthly life rather than the soul's eternal salvation.

Pontifical Biblical Commission (1933)↗

The Pontifical Biblical Commission's answers of 1905-1915 bound Catholics when they were issued (Pius X, Praestantia Scripturae, 1907). In 1955 the Commission's secretary and under-secretary wrote, in an unofficial statement, that scholars are free on such points except where they touch faith and morals. Since 1971 the Commission has been an advisory body. These notes record what was decided and when; they are not badges.

The old texts and the sources

Haydock's preface (1859 text)
This and other titles, with the names of those that wrote the Gospels, are not the words of the Evangelists themselves. The Scripture itself nowhere teacheth us, which books or writings are to be received as true and canonical Scriptures. It is only by the channel of unwritten traditions, and by the testimony and authority of the Catholic Church, that we know and believe that this gospel, for example of S. Matthew, with all contained in it, and that the other books and parts of the Old or New Testament, are of divine authority, or written by divine inspiration; which made S. Augustine say, I should not believe the gospel, were I not moved thereunto by the authority of the Catholic Church: Ego evangelio non crederem, nisi me Ecclesiæ Catholicæ commoveret auctoritas. Lib. con. Epist. Manichæi, quam vocant fundamenti. tom. viii. c. 5, p. 154. A. Ed. Ben. Wi. S. Matthew, author of the gospel that we have under his name, was a Galilean, the son of Alpheus, a Jew, and a tax-gatherer; he was known also by the name of Levi. His vocation happened in the second year of the public ministry of Christ; who, soon after forming the college of his apostles, adopted him into that holy family of the spiritual princes and founders of his Church. Before his departure from Judea, to preach the gospel to distant countries, he yielded to the solicitations of the faithful; and about the eighth year after our Saviour's resurrection, the forty-first of the vulgar era, he began to write his gospel: i.e., the good tidings of salvation to man, through Christ Jesus, our Lord. Of the hagiographers, S. Matthew was the first in the New, as Moses was the first in the Old Testament. And as Moses opened his work with the generation of the heavens and the earth, so S. Matthew begins with the generation of Him, who, in the fulness of time, took upon himself our human nature, to free us from the curse we had brought upon ourselves, and under which the whole creation was groaning. (Haydock) — This holy apostle, after having reaped a great harvest of souls in Judea, preached the faith to the barbarous nations of the East. He was much devoted to heavenly contemplation, and led an austere life; for he eat no flesh, satisfying nature with herbs, roots, seeds, and berries, as Clement of Alexanderia assures us, Pædag. l. ii. c. 1. S. Ambrose says, that God opened to him the country of the Persians. Rufinus and Socrates tell us, that he carried the gospel into Ethiopia, meaning probably the southern or eastern parts of Asia. S. Paulinus informs us, that he ended his course in Parthia; and Venantius Fortunatus says, by martyrdom. — See Butler's Saints' Lives, Septuagint 21st.
Challoner's introduction (1749–52)
Saint Matthew, one of the twelve Apostles, who from being a publican, that is, a taxgatherer, was called by our Saviour to the Apostleship: in that profession his name is Levi. (Luke 5.27, and Mark 2.14.) He was the first of the Evangelists that wrote the Gospel, and that in Hebrew or Syro-Chaldaic which the Jews in Palestine spoke at that time. The original is not now extant; but it was translated in the time of the Apostles into Greek, that version was of equal authority. He wrote about six years after the Lord’s Ascension.
