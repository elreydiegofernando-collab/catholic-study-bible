1 John

At a glance

Author

John the apostle, the author of the Fourth Gospel, as the shared vocabulary of light, darkness, truth, life and love shows. Polycarp echoes it, Papias used it, and Irenaeus quotes it as the work of John the Lord's disciple. The 1913 article rejects the theory of a separate 'John the Elder' as its author.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles of Saint John”, First Epistle: I. Authenticity; IV. Author↗Haydock (1859)↗Challoner↗

Date

Late in the first century. The 1913 article says nothing is certain but the arguments favor the last years of the century; Challoner, following Baronius, gives about sixty-six years after the Ascension, and Haydock calls the time and place uncertain.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles of Saint John”, First Epistle: V. Time and Place↗Challoner↗Haydock (1859)↗

Setting

Probably Ephesus, during John's stay in the province of Asia (Irenaeus, cited in the 1913 article).

About this book

Audience

An encyclical letter to the churches John had evangelized, his 'little children'. Haydock notes it was sometimes called the Epistle to the Parthians.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles of Saint John”, First Epistle: VI. Destination and Purpose↗Haydock (1859)↗

Purpose

The same as the Gospel's: that believers may know they have eternal life through faith in the Son of God (5:13; John 20:31). Haydock adds that it defends the Incarnation against Cerinthus, who denied Christ's divinity, and Basilides, who denied his true body.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles of Saint John”, First Epistle: VI. Destination and Purpose↗Haydock (1859)↗

Key themes

  • What we have seen and touched of the Word of life (1:1-4)
  • God is light: walk in the light and confess your sins (1:5-2:2)
  • The commandment of love (2:7-11; 4:21)
  • Antichrists who deny that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh (2:18-22; 4:2-3)
  • God is love, and we are his children (3:1-2; 4:7-16)
  • The witness of the water and the blood; sin unto death and not unto death (5:6-8, 16-17)

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles of Saint John”, First Epistle: VII. Argument↗Challoner↗

Structure

The 1913 article warns against a strict outline: John builds his thought by returning to a few great ideas. After an introduction (1:1-4) the letter turns on two themes, God is light (1:5-2:29) and God is love (3:1-5:12), with a conclusion on faith and prayer (5:13-21).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles of Saint John”, First Epistle: VII. Argument↗

How the Church reads it

The Catechism takes 'God is love' (4:8, 16) as the revelation of God's inmost life (CCC 221), reads 1:8-9 for confessing our sins to receive mercy (CCC 1847) and 5:16-17 for the distinction of mortal and venial sin (CCC 1854). It reads 4:2-3 as the mark of true faith in the Incarnation against the early denials of Christ's flesh (CCC 463, 465) and 4:10 of Christ as the expiation for our sins (CCC 457). Challoner says the letter confirms the two chief mysteries, the Trinity and the Incarnation.

In the canon

Protocanonical: the Fathers never doubted it, and the 1913 article says Trent only confirmed what had always been accepted. The 'three witnesses in heaven' (5:7) are a separate question (open questions and history notes).

The old texts and the sources

Haydock's preface (1859 text)
This epistle was always acknowledged for canonical, and written by S. John, the apostle and evangelist. At what time and place, is uncertain. It is sometimes called the Epistle to the Parthians, or Persians. The chief design is to set forth the mystery of Christ's incarnation against Cerinthus, who denied Christ's divinity, and against Basilides, who denied that Christ had a true body; with zealous exhortations to love God and our neighbour. (Witham) — The same vein of divine love and charity towards our neighbour which runs throughout the gospel, written by the beloved disciple and evangelist, S. John, is found also in his epistles. He confirms the two principal mysteries of our faith: the mystery of the Trinity, the mystery of the incarnation of Jesus Christ, the Son of God. The sublimity and excellence of the evangelical doctrine he declares: "And this commandment we have from God, that he who loveth God love also his brother;" (C. iv. 21.) and again, "For this is the charity of God, that we keep his commandments; and his commandments are not heavy." C. v. 3. He shews how to distinguish the children of God from those of the devil; marks out those who should be called antichrists; describes the turpitude and gravity of sin. Finally, he shews how the sinner may hope for pardon. It was written, according to Baronius's account, sixty-six years after our Lord's ascension. (Challoner) — The effect of all is to prove the certainty of the Catholic faith, and to renounce all heretics and schismatics, who entice persons from the true saving faith.
