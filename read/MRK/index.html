Mark

16 chapters

At a glance

Author

Mark, the disciple and interpreter of Peter, who set down what Peter preached. Papias, Irenaeus, Clement of Alexandria, Origen, Tertullian and Jerome all say so, and Jerome adds that Peter approved the work for reading in the churches. Haydock records that later tradition made Mark bishop of Alexandria and a martyr there in A.D. 68.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Gospel of Saint Mark”, II. Authorship↗Challoner↗Haydock (1859)↗

Date

The 1913 article concludes it was probably written between A.D. 50 and 67, before Peter's death and before Jerusalem fell. Challoner gives about ten years after the Ascension, in line with the date of A.D. 43 in Eusebius's Chronicle; Irenaeus alone seems to place it after Peter and Paul died.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Gospel of Saint Mark”, V. Place and Date of Composition↗Challoner↗

Setting

Rome, where Mark wrote at the request of Christians who had heard Peter preach. The 1913 article calls the Roman origin certain and dismisses the idea that it was written in Egypt.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Gospel of Saint Mark”, II; V↗Challoner↗Haydock (1859)↗

About this book

Audience

Christians at Rome, mostly of Gentile background. Mark translates Aramaic words, explains Jewish customs, counts money in Roman coin, uses Latin loanwords, and names Rufus, probably because Roman readers knew him (15:21; compare Romans 16:13).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Gospel of Saint Mark”, VI. Destination and Purpose↗

Purpose

To keep Peter's preaching in writing for the Roman Church, and to show readers, believers and inquirers alike, that Jesus is the almighty Son of God. Mark proves this less by quoting prophecy than by telling of Christ's power over nature, sickness and demons (1:1; 15:39).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Gospel of Saint Mark”, VI. Destination and Purpose↗

Key themes

  • Jesus the Son of God, from the first verse to the centurion's confession (1:1; 15:39)
  • Christ's power shown in miracles, nearly a quarter of the Gospel
  • Vivid, eyewitness detail that points to Peter as the source
  • The slowness and weakness of the Twelve, told candidly
  • The way to the cross and the empty tomb, then the mission to the whole world (16:15-20)

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Gospel of Saint Mark”, I. Contents; II. Authorship↗

Structure

A short opening on the Baptist, Christ's baptism and temptation (1:1-13); the ministry in Galilee and nearby lands (1:14-9:49); the ministry in Judea and Perea and the road to Jerusalem (10:1-11:10); the last week, Passion and Resurrection (11:11-16:8); and the appearances, Ascension and mission (16:9-20).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Gospel of Saint Mark”, I. Contents, Selection and Arrangement↗

How the Church reads it

Trent names Mark 6:13 as the place where the anointing of the sick was first hinted at, before James made it known (Session XIV, Extreme Unction, ch. I; compare CCC 1511). The Church reads the long ending as Scripture: 16:15-16 for the mission and the need of baptism (CCC 1223, 1257) and 16:17-18 for the signs of healing (CCC 1507). Trent also counts Mark among the evangelists whose words of institution (14:22-24) carry their plain meaning (Session XIII, ch. I).

Council of Trent, Session XIV, On the Sacrament of Extreme Unction, ch. I↗CCC 1223↗CCC 1257↗CCC 1507↗CCC 1511↗Council of Trent, Session XIII, Decree on the Eucharist, ch. I↗

In the canon

Protocanonical. The closing section, 16:9-20, is one of three New Testament passages that were once disputed; since Trent, Catholics may not question its inspiration.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Canon of the Holy Scriptures”↗Council of Trent, Session IV↗

Historical notes

Historical note26 June 1912 · Pontifical Biblical Commission

Quaestiones de evangeliis secundum Marcum et secundum Lucam (On the Gospels according to Mark and Luke)

AAS 4 (1912) 463-465

Held that Mark, the disciple and interpreter of Peter, wrote the Gospel, and that the arguments against 16:9-20 prove neither that the verses are uninspired nor that Mark did not write them. It kept the traditional order Matthew, Mark, Luke, ruled out dates after the siege of Jerusalem began, and held that Mark wrote according to Peter's preaching with other trustworthy sources.

Pontifical Biblical Commission (1912)↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Gospel of Saint Luke (Biblical Commission)”↗

Historical note26 June 1912 · Pontifical Biblical Commission

De quaestione synoptica (On the Synoptic question)

AAS 4 (1912) 465

Allowed free discussion of how the first three Gospels depend on one another or on oral or written tradition, but held the two-source theory untenable.

Pontifical Biblical Commission (1912)↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Synoptics (Biblical Commission)”↗

The Pontifical Biblical Commission's answers of 1905-1915 bound Catholics when they were issued (Pius X, Praestantia Scripturae, 1907). In 1955 the Commission's secretary and under-secretary wrote, in an unofficial statement, that scholars are free on such points except where they touch faith and morals. Since 1971 the Commission has been an advisory body. These notes record what was decided and when; they are not badges.

The old texts and the sources

Haydock's preface (1859 text)
S. Mark, who wrote this Gospel, is called by S. Augustine, the abridger of S. Matthew; by S. Irenæus, the disciple and interpreter of S. Peter; and according to Origen and S. Jerom, he is the same Mark whom S. Peter calls his son. Stilting, the Bollandist, (in the life of S. John Mark, T. vii. Sep. 27, p. 387, who was son of the sister of S. Barnabas) endeavours to prove that this was the same person as our evangelist; and this is the sentiment of S. Jerom, and some others: but the general opinion is that John, surnamed Mark, mentioned in Acts xii. was a different person. He was the disciple of S. Paul, and companion of S. Barnabas, and was with S. Paul at Antioch, when our evangelist was with S. Peter at Rome, or at Alexandria, as Eusebius, S. Jerom, Baronius, and others observe. Tirinus is of opinion that the evangelist was not one of the seventy-two disciples, because as S. Peter calls him his son, he was converted by S. Peter after the death of Christ. S. Epiphanius, however, assures us he was one of the seventy-two, and forsook Christ after hearing his discourse on the Eucharist, (John vi.) but was converted by S. Peter after Christ's resurrection, hær. 51, c. v. p. 528. — The learned are generally of opinion, that the original was written in Greek, and not in Latin; for, though it was written at the request of the Romans, the Greek language was commonly understood amongst them; and the style itself sufficiently shews this to have been the case: — Omnia Græcè; Cum sit turpe magis nostris nescire Latinè. Juvenal, Satyr vi. The old MS. in Latin, kept at Venice, and supposed by some to be the original, is shewn by Montfaucon and other antiquaries, to have been written in the sixth century, and contains the oldest copy extant of S. Jerom's version. — S. Peter revised the work of S. Mark, approved of it, and authorized it to be read in the religious assemblies of the faithful; hence some, as we learn from Tertullian, attributed this gospel to S. Peter himself. S. Mark relates the same facts as S. Matthew, and often in the same words: but he adds several particular circumstances, and changes the order of the narration, in which he agrees with S. Luke and S. John. He narrates two histories not mentioned by S. Matthew; the widow's two mites, and Christ's appearing to the two disciples on the road to Emmaus; also some miraculous cures; (Mark i. 40, vii. 32, viii. 22, 26) and omits many things noticed by S. Matthew. . . But nothing proves clearly, as Dom. Ceillier and others suppose, that he made use of S. Matthew's gospel. In his narrative he is concise, and he writes with a most pleasing simplicity and elegance. It is certain that S. Mark was sent by S. Peter into Egypt, and was by him appointed bishop of Alexandria, (which, after Rome, was accounted the second city of the world) as Eusebius, S. Epiphanius, S. Jerom, and others assure us. He remained here, governing that flourishing church with great prudence, zeal, and sanctity. He suffered martyrdom in the 14th year of the reign of Nero, in the year of Christ 68, and three years after the death of SS. Peter and Paul, at Alexandria, on the 25th of April; having been seized the previous day, which was Sunday, at the altar, as he was offering to God the prayer of the oblation, or the mass.
Challoner's introduction (1749–52)
St. Mark, the disciple and interpreter of St. Peter (saith St. Jerome), according to what he heard from Peter himself, wrote at Rome a brief Gospel at the request of the Brethren, about ten years after our lord’s Ascension; which when Peter had heard, he approved of it and with his authority published it to the church to be read. Baronius and others say that the original was written in Latin: but the more general opinion is that the Evangelist wrote it in Greek.
