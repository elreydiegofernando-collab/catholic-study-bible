Micah

Douay: Micheas · 7 chapters

At a glance

Author

Micheas of Morasthi, near Geth in Juda, a contemporary of Isaias whom Challoner says he resembles in spirit and style; Challoner distinguishes him from Micheas son of Jemla, who lived under Achab (3 Kings 22). The 1913 article, by Van Hoonacker, defends his authorship of the whole book against critics who assign parts of 4-7 to later writers.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Micheas (Micah)”, III. Micheas, Minor Prophet↗Challoner, Introduction↗

Date

The title names Joatham, Achaz and Ezechias (about 740-698 B.C.), but the 1913 article thinks none of his surviving addresses is earlier than Ezechias; it dates chapters 4-5 shortly after the fall of Samaria in 722 B.C.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Micheas (Micah)”, III. Micheas, Minor Prophet↗

Setting

Jerusalem in the Assyrian crisis that destroyed Samaria and threatened Juda.

About this book

Audience

Haydock (citing Calmet) says he addresses both Israel and Juda: their princes, priests, prophets and judges above all.

Haydock (1859), Preface↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Micheas (Micah)”, III. Micheas, Minor Prophet↗

Purpose

To announce judgment on Samaria and Jerusalem for their sins, even that Sion shall be plowed like a field (3:12), and then the Messianic peace, the nations streaming to the Lord's house, and the ruler to come from Bethlehem.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Micheas (Micah)”, III. Micheas, Minor Prophet↗Haydock (1859), Preface↗

Key themes

  • Judgment on rulers, priests and false prophets who exploit the people.
  • The nations go up to the mountain of the Lord; swords are beaten into plowshares (4:1-4).
  • The ruler of Israel to be born in Bethlehem (5:2 Douay).
  • What the Lord requires: 'to do judgment, and to love mercy, and to walk solicitous with thy God' (6:8).
  • Hope in God's mercy for the fallen (7).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Micheas (Micah)”, III. Micheas, Minor Prophet↗CCC 762↗Matt 2:5-6

Structure

The 1913 article finds three parts: threats against Samaria and Juda (1-3); the Messianic peace and the king from Bethlehem (4-5); and God's dramatic lawsuit with his people, ending in a prayer of hope (6-7).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Micheas (Micah)”, III. Micheas, Minor Prophet↗

How the Church reads it

When Herod asked where the Christ was to be born, the chief priests answered from Micheas: in Bethlehem of Juda (Matt 2:5-6). The Catechism counts the prophets' vision of all nations gathered to the Lord among the preparations for the gathering of the People of God into the Church (CCC 762). Haydock (after Calmet) says the happiness promised after the captivity is a figure of what the Church would enjoy.

Matt 2:5-6CCC 762↗Haydock (1859), Preface↗

In the canon

One of the twelve minor prophets in Trent's list (Session IV) and in the Catechism's (CCC 120); Haydock notes that Sirach already speaks of 'the twelve' as a collection (Ecclesiasticus 49:12 Douay).

Council of Trent, Session IV, p. 18↗CCC 120↗Haydock (1859), Preface↗

The old texts and the sources

Haydock's preface (1859 text)
Micheas, of Morasti, a little town in the tribe of Juda, was cotemporary with the prophet Isaias, whom he resembles both in his spirit and his style. He is different from the prophet Micheas, mentioned in the Third Book of Kings, (C. xxii.) for that Micheas lived in the days of king Achab, one hundred and fifty years before the time of Ezechias, under whom this Micheas prophesied, (Challoner) as he did in the two preceding reigns. (Haydock) — He addresses both Israel and Juda, and predicts the happiness of the Jews after the captivity, as a figure of what the church should enjoy. (Calmet) — The Jews shall embrace the faith at last, after the Gentiles. (Worthington) — We have only a small part of the predictions of Micheas, though he may have written no more. His style is obscure, like that of Osee. (Calmet) — His name signifies "humility," or "who is like." (St. Jerome)
