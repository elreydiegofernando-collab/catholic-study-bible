1 Maccabees†

Douay: 1 Machabees · 16 chapters · deuterocanonical

At a glance

Author

The author is unknown. The 1913 article describes him as a Palestinian Jew, deeply religious, zealous for the Law and devoted to the Machabean cause, and finds no ground for calling him a Sadducee.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Books of Machabees”, The First Book of Machabees, Author and Date of Composition↗Challoner, Introduction↗

Date

The 1913 article dates it after John Hyrcanus came to power (135 B.C.) and before Pompey took Jerusalem (63 B.C.), most likely in the last years of the 2nd century B.C. It survives in Greek, but the article holds it was first written in Hebrew; St. Jerome says he saw it in Hebrew.

Setting

Judea under the Seleucid kings, from Antiochus Epiphanes' persecution and the profanation of the Temple to the rule of Simon and the accession of John Hyrcanus: the years 175-135 B.C.

About this book

Audience

The Jewish people after the Machabean wars, for whom it records how their freedom of worship and their nation were won back.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Books of Machabees”, The First Book of Machabees, Contents↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Books of Machabees”, The First Book of Machabees, Character↗

Purpose

The 1913 article calls it a history of the Jewish people's struggle for religious and political liberty under the Machabee family, written in the calm, objective manner of the earlier historical books. Its events are carefully dated by the Seleucid era.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Books of Machabees”, The First Book of Machabees, Contents↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Books of Machabees”, The First Book of Machabees, Character↗

Key themes

  • Fidelity to the Law under persecution.
  • The profanation and rededication of the Temple (the feast of the Dedication).
  • Zeal of Mathathias and the courage of Judas Machabeus.
  • God's help to those who trust him, often spoken of reverently as 'heaven'.
  • Leadership passed from brother to brother: Judas, Jonathan, Simon.

Structure

The 1913 article finds an introduction on the passage from Persian to Seleucid rule (1:1-9), the rising under Mathathias (1:10-2:70), the deeds and death of Judas (3:1-9:22), the leadership of Jonathan (9:23 to the end of 12) and of Simon (13:1-16:17), and a short close on John Hyrcanus (16:18-24).

How the Church reads it

Challoner, citing St. Augustine, stresses that the Church received these books on the authority of apostolic tradition, and Haydock notes that St. Clement, St. Cyprian and St. Ambrose quoted them as divine. The feast of the Dedication instituted after the Temple was cleansed (4:59) is the feast Jesus attended in Jerusalem (John 10:22).

Challoner, Introduction↗Haydock (1859), Preface↗John 10:22

In the canon

A deuterocanonical book. Challoner, citing St. Augustine (City of God 18.36), notes that the Jews do not receive it but the Church does, guided by the tradition received from the apostles, and that two general councils, Florence and Trent, declared both books canonical (Trent, Session IV; CCC 120). The so-called Third and Fourth Books of Machabees are not canonical (1913 article; Haydock).

The old texts and the sources

Haydock's preface (1859 text)
These Books are so called, because they contain the history of the people of God under the command of Judas Machabeus and his brethren; and he, as some will have it, was surnamed Machabeus from carrying on his ensigns, or standards, those words of Exodus xv. 11. "Who is like to thee among the strong, O Lord;" in which the initial letters, in the Hebrew, are M. C. B. E. I. It is not known who was the author of these books. But as to their authority, though they are not received by the Jews, saith S. Augustine, (l. xviii. de civ. Dei. c. xxxvi.) they are received by the Church; who, in settling her canon of the Scriptures, chose rather to be directed by the tradition she had received from the apostles of Christ, than by that of the Scribes and Pharisees. And as the Church has declared these two books canonical, even in two general councils, viz. Florence and Trent, there can be no doubt of their authenticity. (Challoner) — The most ancient Father, Clem. (Strom. i.) S. Cyprian, (1 ep. iii. ad. Cornel.) S. Ambrose, (Off. i. 41. &c.) quote them as divine; and the third Council of Carthage, Trent, &c. have inserted them in the canon. Though (Worthington) S. Jerom did not translate (Calmet) or urge them against the Jews, he had a great regard for them. In Dan. i. and xi. &c. The texts to which Protestants object, will be explained. They are not more difficult than many which occur in the Books of Kings and Paralipomenon. Other books have been formerly contested, which they now admit. The author of the second books seems to have designed at first only to insert two supplements. He then resolved to abridge the work of Jason, and hence added a preface, (C. ii. 20.) which may be first perused. He then gives an account of some who had suffered death for the truth; and in the eighth and following chapters, the victories of the Machabees, which had been partly recorded in the first book, are specified, with some fresh circumstances. Judas was styled the Machabee for his strength and valour, (Worthington) being "the scourge" of God, (Haydock) or because he was an exterminator. (Menochius) — Yet the etymology is not well ascertained. That given above, (Zac. xii. 5.; Haydock) or M. C. B. I. (Buxt. Prideaux, &c.) seems to be overthrown, by the Syriac having always k instead of c, which is the case in two Chaldee MSS. These may not contain a truer history, but they rectify several mistakes in the printed copies. (Kennicott) — S. Jerom found the first of these books in Hebrew the second in Greek. Ep. 106. We have a third also in Greek, in the Comp. Bible; as well as a fourth, mentioned by Sixtus, (Bib. i. W.) "on the administration of Hircan," (Calmet) to which reference seems to be made 1 Mac. xv. 24. (Worthington) though what is extant be a work of no authority. "The empire of reason," falsely attributed to Josephus, and printed among his works, is more probably the fourth book, known to the ancients. Neither of these latter were ever deemed canonical by any authentic decree. (Worthington) — Many have indeed ranked the with the other contested works: but now it is generally agreed that they belong not to the sacred Scriptures, as the two former do. On these (Calmet) indeed, as almost on all the sacred books, (Haydock) authors have varied: but he most respectable and the most numerous declare in their favour. To arrange the four books in chronological order, we must make the first and third exchange places. This last can obtain its title only inasmuch as the Jews of Alexandria, who were persecuted by Philopator, and miraculously delivered, were animated with the same sentiments of piety and heroism as the rest of the Machabees. C. See Ecclesiasticus l. 1. 21. 23. (Haydock) — The authors of the first and second books relate many of the same events; yet seem not to have seen each other's works. The latter follows the Greek chronology, dating from Tisri, Septuagint and October, (Calmet) A. 3828 to 3843, (Haydock) while the former dates from Nisan, (Calmet) our March or April, A. 3829 to 3869. The third book gives an account of Philopator's persecution in Egypt, A. 3787. The fourth, A. 3869 to 3698, (Haydock) is little known among the Latins. Calmet gives two copies, the one regarding Hircan and the other "the government of reason," or the martyrdom of the Machabees, A. 3837; (Haydock) the author of which last seems to have been a Stoic. (Calmet) — We shall pass them over, with Protestants, as they are not received by the Catholic Church. (Haydock)
