Baruch†

6 chapters · deuterocanonical

At a glance

Author

Baruch, son of Nerias, Jeremias's secretary and disciple, by the book's title and tradition; Challoner notes that because of this link the Fathers often quoted it under Jeremias's name. The 1913 article treats chapter 6 as a separate writing whose title names Jeremias, which many critics, after St. Jerome's word 'pseudepigraphos', deny is his.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Baruch”↗Challoner, Introduction↗Haydock (1859), Preface↗

Date

The book says it was written in Babylon in the fifth year after the burning of Jerusalem, about 582 B.C. The 1913 article reports that non-Catholic critics date the compiler much later, some even after A.D. 70, a date it calls easily disproved.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Baruch”↗

Setting

Babylon, among the exiles of King Jechonias, who send the book with offerings to Jerusalem; chapter 6 warns the deportees against the idols they will see there.

About this book

Audience

The exiles in Babylon and the remnant in Jerusalem, called to confess their sins, seek wisdom in the Law and take courage.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Baruch”↗

Purpose

The 1913 article finds an introduction (1:1-14), a confession of the sins that caused the exile with a prayer for pardon (1:15-3:8), a praise of the Wisdom given to Israel in the Law (3:9-4:4), and four odes of comfort beginning 'Take courage' (4:5-5:9).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Baruch”↗

Key themes

  • Confession of sin and prayer for mercy.
  • Wisdom found in the Law given to Israel (3:9-4:4).
  • God's wisdom appearing on earth among men (3:38).
  • Comfort and the promised return to Jerusalem.
  • The folly of idols (6).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Baruch”↗Haydock (1859), Preface↗CCC 2112↗

Structure

Introduction (1:1-14); confession and prayer (1:15-3:8); the praise of Wisdom (3:9-4:4); odes of comfort (4:5-5:9); and, as an appendix, the Epistle of Jeremias against idolatry (6).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Baruch”↗

How the Church reads it

Haydock reports St. Jerome's testimony that the book 'contains many things relating to Christ and the latter times', and notes that the Church still read Baruch in its liturgy under Jeremias's name (he cites a vigil prophecy, 'Sab. Pent. proph. 6'). Challoner reads 3:38, where wisdom 'was seen upon earth, and conversed with men', of the Incarnation, and Haydock adds that most of the Fathers prove Christ's divinity from it, citing Tertullian, St. Cyprian and Eusebius. The Catechism cites the Epistle of Jeremias (chapter 6) in its teaching against idolatry (CCC 2112).

In the canon

A deuterocanonical book. The 1913 article notes that the Council of Trent defined it 'sacred and canonical'; Trent's list reads 'Jeremias, with Baruch' (Session IV), and Haydock adds the councils of Laodicea and Florence. The article also holds that chapter 6 has always been recognized by the Church as inspired.

The old texts and the sources

Haydock's preface (1859 text)
Baruch was a man of noble extraction, and learned in the law, secretary and disciple of the prophet Jeremias, and a sharer in his labours and persecutions; which is the reason why the ancient Fathers have considered this book as a part of the prophecy of Jeremias, and have usually quoted it under his name. (Challoner) — The frequent Hebrew idioms shew it to have been originally in that language. Baruch wrote by inspiration of the Holy Ghost the letter comprising the five first chapters, which he carried to the Jews from their brethren at Babylon. The martyrologies place his death, Dec. 28. The sixth chapter contains a letter of Jeremias, to which allusion is clearly made 2 Mac. ii. 2. The Church still recites the works of Baruch under the name of Jeremias. Sab. Pent. proph. 6. (Calmet) — Many Fathers did so formerly, though they doubted not but Baruch was the author. See S. Iræn. v. 35. S. Aug. de Civ. Dei. xviii. 33. &c. Others, with Origen (Prin. ii. 3.) specify the writer; and the Councils of Laodicea, Florence, and Trent, decide that it is canonical. S. Jerom alleged it not against the Jews, as they denied its authority. (Worthington) — See Jer. xxxii. 44. (Haydock) — In his preface on Jeremias he testifies that "it contains many things relating to Christ and the latter times." (Worthington) — Grotius in vain attempts to represent some parts as interpolated (C. iii. 38. &c. C.) and L. Cappellus has left some posthumous notes, which would represent the author as a stupid impostor, though he acknowledges his great learning. (Houbigant)
