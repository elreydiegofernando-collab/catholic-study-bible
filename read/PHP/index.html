Philippians

4 chapters

At a glance

Author

Paul, with Timothy named in the greeting (1:1); the 1913 article thinks Timothy probably wrote it down at Paul's dictation. Its authenticity is admitted almost universally.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistle to the Philippians”, I. Historical Circumstances; III. Authenticity↗

Date

During Paul's captivity at Rome. The 1913 article gives 62-64 and its author places it toward the end of the captivity; Haydock gives about 62, and Challoner about twenty-nine years after the Ascension.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistle to the Philippians”, Place and Date↗Haydock (1859)↗Challoner↗

Setting

Rome: Paul mentions the praetorium (1:13) and 'Caesar's household' (4:22). The 1913 article calls the Roman setting nearly unanimous, even among those who put the other captivity letters at Caesarea.

About this book

Audience

The church at Philippi in Macedonia, with its bishops and deacons (1:1), the first Macedonians to receive the faith (Acts 16) and long devoted to Paul.

Challoner↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistle to the Philippians”, I. Historical Circumstances↗

Purpose

To thank the Philippians for the gift Epaphroditus brought, to send him home after a serious illness, and to urge unity, humility and joy. Paul also warns them against Judaizing teachers (3:2-21).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistle to the Philippians”, I. Historical Circumstances, Occasion, and Character↗Challoner↗Haydock (1859)↗

Key themes

  • Joy in the Lord, even in chains (1:12-26; 4:4)
  • Christ who emptied himself, took the form of a servant, and was exalted (2:5-11)
  • Humility and concord, including a plea to Evodia and Syntyche (2:1-4; 4:2-3)
  • Righteousness through faith in Christ rather than the Law (3:7-11)
  • Pressing on toward the prize of God's call (3:12-14)

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistle to the Philippians”, II. Analysis↗

Structure

No strict plan, being an outpouring of the heart. Greeting and thanksgiving (1:1-11); news of Paul and exhortations to unity built on Christ's example (1:12-2:18); plans for Timothy and Epaphroditus (2:19-30); warning against the Judaizers (3:1-4:1); final exhortations (4:2-9); thanks for the gift and greetings (4:10-23).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistle to the Philippians”, II. Analysis↗

How the Church reads it

The Church sings the hymn of 2:5-11 as a canticle in the Liturgy of the Hours and reads it as the mystery of the Incarnation (CCC 461), and as confessing Jesus as Lord with the honor due to God (CCC 449). The Catechism also draws on 1:21-23 for the Christian meaning of death as being with Christ (CCC 1010, 1025).

In the canon

Protocanonical; Polycarp, the Muratorian Canon, Irenaeus, Clement of Alexandria and Tertullian all attribute it to Paul.

The old texts and the sources

Haydock's preface (1859 text)
Philippi, a considerable city in Macedonia, so called from Philip, father of Alexander the Great. S. Paul had preached there. Acts xvi. Those people had a great veneration for him, and supplied his wants when he was at Corinth, and again when he was a prisoner at Rome, sending to him by Epaphroditus, who is thought to have been the bishop of Philippi. S. Paul sent this letter by him to the Philippians, (written during his imprisonment) from Rome; but whether during his first or second imprisonment, is uncertain. (Witham) — It is generally believed that S. Paul wrote it about the year 62, in his first confinement. In it he testifies to the faithful his most tender gratitude and acknowledgement for the assistance they had sent him, and a zeal the most ardent for their salvation. He felicitates them on their courage under sufferings for the cause of Jesus Christ, on their good works also, and forcibly excites them to confidence and joy. — The Philippians were the first among the Macedonians converted to the faith. S. Paul, in this epistle, recommends charity, unity, and humility; and warns them against false teachers, whom he calls dogs, and enemies of the cross of Christ. He also returns thanks for their benefactions. It was written about twenty-nine years after our Lord's ascension. (Challoner)
Challoner's introduction (1749–52)
The Philippians were the first among the Macedonians converted to the faith. He also returns thanks for their benefactions. It was written about twenty-nine years after our Lord’s Ascension.
