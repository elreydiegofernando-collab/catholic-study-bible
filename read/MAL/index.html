Douay: Malachias · 4 chapters

At a glance

Author

The 1913 article, by Van Hoonacker, holds 'Malachias' to be a proper name and says all that is known of him is his preaching and approximate time. It reports that Jewish schools early identified him with Esdras, an identification St. Jerome explains and the article calls without historical value; Challoner says some believe he was Esdras.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Malachias”↗Challoner, Introduction↗Haydock (1859), Preface↗

Date

The 1913 article says critics practically agree on the middle of the 5th century B.C., and places it shortly before Nehemias's mission: the Persian governor, a Temple already standing with worship grown lax, and the abuses Nehemias later fought all point there. Challoner says about four hundred years before Christ.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Malachias”↗Challoner, Introduction↗

Setting

Jerusalem after the return, with the second Temple standing but its priests careless, its offerings blemished, tithes withheld and marriages with foreign women multiplying.

About this book

Audience

The priests and the people of Juda: the 1913 article finds the first part aimed at the priests and the people's infidelities, the second at those who doubted God's justice.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Malachias”↗

Purpose

To recall priests and people to faithful worship, sincere offerings, the marriage covenant and the tithes, and to announce the messenger who will prepare the Lord's way and the day of judgment. Challoner sums up: he foretells the coming of Christ, the rejection of the Jewish sacrifices, and the calling of the Gentiles to offer an acceptable sacrifice in every place.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Malachias”↗Challoner, Introduction↗

Key themes

  • God's love for his people (1:2).
  • A clean oblation offered to God's name in every place among the Gentiles (1:11).
  • The priest's lips should keep knowledge (2:7).
  • Faithfulness to the wife of one's youth; the 1913 article reads 2:16, obscure in the text, as disapproving the divorce Deuteronomy tolerated.
  • The messenger and Elias who come before the day of the Lord (3:1; 4:5-6).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Malachias”↗CCC 1350↗CCC 2643↗CCC 1540↗CCC 1611↗CCC 718↗

Structure

The 1913 article finds two parts: 1:2-2:16, against the priests' faults in sacrifice and teaching and the people's infidelities; and 2:17 to the end, on the coming messenger and judgment, closing with the call to remember the law of Moses and the promise of Elias.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Malachias”↗

How the Church reads it

The Council of Trent teaches that the Mass is the clean oblation the Lord foretold by Malachias (Session XXII, ch. 1), and the Catechism repeats that the Church alone offers this pure oblation (CCC 1350, 2643); the 1913 article argues that 1:11 is a Messianic prophecy of a true sacrifice. The Gospels identify the messenger and Elias with St. John the Baptist (Matt 11:10, 14; Luke 1:17), as the Catechism also does (CCC 718).

In the canon

One of the twelve minor prophets in Trent's list (Session IV) and in the Catechism's (CCC 120); Haydock notes that Sirach already speaks of 'the twelve' as a collection (Ecclesiasticus 49:12 Douay). It closes the collection of the twelve, and its author has long been regarded as the last of the canonical prophets of the Old Testament.

Council of Trent, Session IV, p. 18↗CCC 120↗Haydock (1859), Preface↗

The old texts and the sources

Haydock's preface (1859 text)
Malachias, whose name signifies "the angel of the Lord," was contemporary with Nehemias, and by some is believed to have been the same person with Esdras. He was the last of the prophets, in the order of time, and flourished about four hundred years before Christ. He foretells the coming of Christ; the reprobation of the Jews and their sacrifices; and the calling of the Gentiles, who shall offer up to God in every place an acceptable sacrifice. (Challoner) — He also clearly speaks of the twofold coming of Christ, preceded by the Baptist and by Elias. Nothing is known for certain respecting this prophet. He inveighs against the same crimes as Nehemias, to whose covenant he alludes, C. ii. 4. None was afterwards recognized for a prophet till the Baptist appeared. (Calmet) — Both priests and people are here reproved, and the Jewish law yields to that of Christ. (Worthington) — No date is prefixed no more than to the works of Jonas, Nahum, &c. S. Jerom seems to fix on the seventh year of Artaxerxes, when Esrdras came to Jerusalem. Liber ejus pro titulo sit. (Haydock)
