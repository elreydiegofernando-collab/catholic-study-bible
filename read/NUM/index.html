Numbers

36 chapters

At a glance

Author

Tradition ascribes Numbers to Moses, and the book says Moses wrote down the stages of Israel's journey (33:2), a passage the 1913 article cites as direct witness to his writing. Haydock, quoting Calmet, says Moses composed Numbers and Deuteronomy a little before his death, from records he had carefully kept.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Pentateuch”, III. Authenticity, A. Testimony of Sacred Scripture, (1) Witness of the Pentateuch↗Haydock (1859), Preface↗

Date

Dated with the rest of the Pentateuch: Moses' lifetime by tradition (15th or 13th century B.C.), near the end of the forty years in the desert according to Haydock's source Calmet. The 1906 decree allowed later inspired additions.

Setting

The desert between Sinai and the Jordan. Challoner says the book covers almost thirty-nine years, from the second month of the second year after leaving Egypt to the eleventh month of the fortieth; it ends on the plains of Moab, east of the Jordan, with the promised land in view.

About this book

Audience

Israel, the people whose desert history it records; it closes with laws for dividing the land, addressed to the generation about to enter it.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Pentateuch”, II. Analysis, D. Numbers↗

Purpose

It records Israel's years in the desert: the census and ordering of the tribes, the departure from Sinai, the rebellions that cost a whole generation its entry into the land, and the march to the Jordan. The 1913 article notes that the thirty-eight years of the condemned generation are passed over briefly, and Haydock stresses how God protected Moses against every opponent, Balaam included.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Pentateuch”, II. Analysis, D. Numbers↗Haydock (1859), Preface↗

Key themes

  • Order in God's camp: the census and the tribes arranged around the tabernacle.
  • Murmuring and rebellion (the spies, Core), met with judgment and mercy.
  • The bronze serpent lifted up for healing.
  • Balaam's oracle of the star that shall rise out of Jacob.
  • The seventy elders who share in Moses' spirit.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Pentateuch”, II. Analysis, D. Numbers↗Haydock (1859), Preface↗CCC 2130↗CCC 528↗CCC 1541↗

Structure

The 1913 article finds three parts: (1) 1:1-14:45, events before the rebellious generation was rejected; (2) 15:1-19:22, laws and events belonging to that rejected generation, including the revolt of Core; (3) 20:1-36:13, the last stage of the journey, the second census, the conquest east of the Jordan, the list of encampments and the boundaries of the land. It notes that the writer tells of the census before the departure from Sinai, out of time order, so as not to break the travel story.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Pentateuch”, II. Analysis, D. Numbers↗

How the Church reads it

The Church's liturgy sees in the seventy elders who received a share of Moses' spirit a figure of the ordained ministry (CCC 1541). The Catechism counts the bronze serpent among the images God allowed that pointed to salvation through the incarnate Word (CCC 2130), as Jesus applies it to his own lifting up (John 3:14). Balaam's star is read with the Epiphany, where the Magi stand for the first gentiles coming to Christ (CCC 528).

In the canon

Numbers is the fourth of the 'five books of Moses' in Trent's list of the canonical Scriptures (Session IV), and it is in the Catechism's list of the canon (CCC 120).

Historical notes

Historical note27 June 1906 · Pontifical Biblical Commission

On the Mosaic authenticity of the Pentateuch (De mosaica authentia Pentateuchi)

ASS 39 (1906) 377

The Commission answered that the critics' arguments did not justify denying that Moses is the author of the Pentateuch. It allowed that he used written or oral sources, that he may have had others write the work under his direction and then approved it, and that the text later received inspired additions, updated wording and copyists' errors.

Pontifical Biblical Commission (1906)↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Pentateuch”, III. Authenticity, D. Ecclesiastical Decisions↗Pius X, Praestantia Scripturae↗J. A. Fitzmyer, on the 1955 clarification↗Pontifical Biblical Commission, documents↗

Historical note16 January 1948 · Pontifical Biblical Commission

Letter to Cardinal Suhard on the sources of the Pentateuch and the literary form of Genesis 1-11

AAS 40 (1948) 45-48

In a letter to the Archbishop of Paris, the Commission said scholars no longer doubt that the Pentateuch drew on sources and that its laws grew over time, and it invited Catholic scholars to study these questions freely. It added that Genesis 1-11 does not fit classical or modern categories of history-writing, yet relates in simple, figurative language the basic truths presupposed by salvation and a popular account of human origins.

Pontifical Biblical Commission (1948)↗

The Pontifical Biblical Commission's answers of 1905-1915 bound Catholics when they were issued (Pius X, Praestantia Scripturae, 1907). In 1955 the Commission's secretary and under-secretary wrote, in an unofficial statement, that scholars are free on such points except where they touch faith and morals. Since 1971 the Commission has been an advisory body. These notes record what was decided and when; they are not badges.

The old texts and the sources

Haydock's preface (1859 text)
This fourth Book of Moses is called Numbers, because it begins with the numbering of the people. The Hebrews, from its first words, call it Vaydedabber. It contains the transactions of the Israelites, from the second month of the second year after their going out of Egypt, until the beginning of the eleventh month of the 40th year; that is, a history almost of thirty-nine years. (Challoner) — In the nine first chapters various orders of people are described, and several laws are given or repeated. From the 10th to the 33d, the marches and history of God's people are related; (Haydock) from the 20th of the second month, in the second year after their departure out of Egypt, till the eleventh month of the 40th year, and the last of Moses: so that this Book contains the transactions of almost thirty-nine years; (Tirinus) whereas, the Book of Leviticus specified only some of the laws and occurrences of one month. Here we behold what opposition Moses experienced from Aaron and his sister, from Core, and from all the people; and yet God protected him, in the midst of all dangers, and confounded, not only their attempts, but those also of Balaam, and of all his external foes. (Haydock) — Moses conquers the Madianites, and divides the conquered country between the tribes of Ruben, Gad, and half of the tribe of Manasses. In the three last chapters, he describes the land of Chanaan, orders all the inhabitants to be exterminated, assigns cities for the Levites, and for refuge; and forbids such marriages, as might cause any confusion in the distribution of the lands belonging to each tribe. Moses composed this part of the Pentateuch, as well as that of Deuteronomy, a little while before his death, out of the memoirs which he had carefully preserved. (Calmet) — According to Usher, the people were numbered this second time, A.M. 2514, C. i.; after which they leave the desert of Sinai, (C. x. 11.) go to Cades-barne, and return thither again 2552. Soon after this, Mary and Aaron die; Moses lifts up the brazen serpent; and the Hebrews take possession of part of the promised land (2553) on the eastern banks of the Jordan. That on the western side, flowing with milk and honey, was conquered by Josue in the following years. (Haydock)
Challoner's introduction (1749–52)
This fourth Book of Moses is called NUMBERS, because it begins with the numbering of the people. The Hebrews, from its first words, call it VAIEDABBER. It contains the transactions of the Israelites from the second month of the second year after their going out of Egypt, until the beginning of the eleventh month of the fortieth year; that is, a history almost of thirty-nine years.
