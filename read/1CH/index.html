1 Chronicles

Douay: 1 Paralipomenon · 29 chapters

At a glance

Author

Older writers generally named Esdras, and the 1913 article says most Catholic authors still hold that view; Challoner thinks the books were abridged from the ancient annals 'by Esdras or some other sacred writer'. Most modern non-Catholic scholars, the article reports, assign them to an unknown writer.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Books of Paralipomenon”, Author and Time of Composition↗Challoner, Introduction↗Haydock (1859), Preface↗

Date

Certainly after the return from exile: David's line is traced past Zorobabel, and David's gifts are reckoned in Persian darics. Catholic authors, the 1913 article says, put it at the end of the 5th or start of the 4th century B.C.; most non-Catholic scholars between 300 and 250 B.C.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Books of Paralipomenon”, Author and Time of Composition↗

Setting

A summary of sacred history from Adam to the end of the Captivity; this first book gives the genealogies (1-9), then Saul's death and the reign of David (10-29).

About this book

Audience

The community restored after the Exile, gathered around the rebuilt temple.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Books of Paralipomenon”, Object↗

Purpose

The 1913 article says the writer did not mean to fill gaps in Kings but to write the religious history of Juda centered on the temple and its worship, and with it the house of David. His practical aim was to urge faithful, exact worship in the restored temple, showing that kings and people prospered when faithful and suffered when not.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Books of Paralipomenon”, Object↗

Key themes

  • The genealogies, with Juda and Levi given first place.
  • David as founder of the temple's worship and of the Levites' service.
  • The ark brought to Sion with song.
  • God's promise of an everlasting house to David.
  • Fidelity in worship brings blessing.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Books of Paralipomenon”, Object↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Books of Paralipomenon”, Contents↗Haydock (1859), Preface↗

Structure

Genealogies from Adam to the families of the Restoration (1-9), Saul's death as a prelude (10), then David's reign, dwelling on the ark, the preparations for the temple and the orders of priests and Levites (11-29).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Books of Paralipomenon”, Contents↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Books of Paralipomenon”, Object↗Haydock (1859), Preface↗

How the Church reads it

Haydock quotes St. Jerome's high praise of the book: an epitome of the Old Testament, without which a reader would in vain try to understand the Scriptures, and which explains many difficulties of the Gospels. Its royal genealogy of David's line is the background to the genealogies of Christ in Matthew and Luke.

Haydock (1859), Preface↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Books of Paralipomenon”↗Matt 1:1-17; Luke 3:23-38

In the canon

This is the first of Trent's 'two of Paralipomenon' (Session IV); the Catechism lists it as 1 Chronicles (CCC 120). The 1913 article says the two books are really one work, split first in the Septuagint; the Hebrew Bible puts it at the end of the Writings, the Greek and Latin after Kings.

Council of Trent, Session IV, p. 18↗CCC 120↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Books of Paralipomenon”, Unity and Places in the Canon↗

The old texts and the sources

Haydock's preface (1859 text)
These Books are called by the Greek Interpreters, Paralipomenon; ( Paraleipomenon,) that is, of things left out, or omitted; because they are a kind of supplement of such things as were passed over in the Books of Kings. The Hebrews call them, Dibre Hajamim; that is, The words of the days, or The Chronicles. Not that they are the books which are so often quoted in the Kings, under the title of, The Words of the days of the kings of Israel, and of the kings of Juda; for the Books of Paralipomenon were written after the Books of Kings; but because, in all probability, they have been abridged from those ancient words of the days, by Esdras, or some other sacred author. (Challoner) — The author of this compilation refers to the same works, 2 Par. xvi. 11. &c. These journals were principally composed by prophets, though there were other people appointed to write the most important occurrences. 2 K. viii. 16. 4 K. xviii. 18. The genealogies of families, particularly of the Levites, and the interests of piety and religion, are kept most in view. (Calmet) — The variations which appear between this work and the other parts of Scripture, are owing to the faults of transcribers; and, though they could not be satisfactorily explained, it would be rashness to condemn the author of inaccuracy, at this distance of time, when we know so little of those transactions. (Haydock) — Who calls in question the history of Alexander, though the different authors of it scarcely agree in one calculation of the number of troops, nations conquered, &c.?" Yet the work before us is of far higher authority, as it was dictated by the Holy Ghost. (Calmet) — "Without it, a person would in vain pretend to understand the Scriptures." It is "an epitome of the Old Testament," and "explains many difficulties of the gospels." (St. Jerome) — The author does not, however, seem to have designed to draw up an exact epitome, or to supply the deficiencies of the other works. (Calmet) — The first nine chapters contain various genealogical histories. In the 10th, we have the election and death of Saul; and in the remainder of the first book, the transactions of David, (Worthington) till the year 2990, where the second book commences with the reign of Solomon, and brings us to the end of the captivity. A.M. 3468. (Calmet)
