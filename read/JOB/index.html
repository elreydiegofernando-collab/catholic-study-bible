Job

42 chapters

At a glance

Author

Challoner says the writer is uncertain: some name Job himself, others Moses or one of the prophets. The 1913 article says the author is unknown and that it is now universally held the book is not earlier than Solomon, though earlier than Ezechiel, who names Job (14:14); many Catholic scholars assign it to Solomon's reign.

Challoner, Introduction↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Job”, XI. Time of Composition↗

Date

The 1913 article dates the book between Solomon's reign and Ezechiel, with many Catholics favoring the age of Solomon and many Protestants a later time; older writers who named Job or Moses put it much earlier. Job himself, the article argues, lived long before Moses, in the age of the patriarchs; Haydock suggests the time of the Egyptian bondage.

Setting

The land of Hus among the 'people of the East', where Job, a wealthy and upright man outside the chosen people, loses everything; Challoner, with the more probable opinion, makes him a descendant of Esau and the same as Jobab, king of Edom (Gen 36:33).

About this book

Audience

The 1913 article says it was written to instruct, to edify by Job's example of patience, and to console all who suffer.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Job”, VI. Design of the Book↗

Purpose

The 1913 article says its main subject is the problem of evil, especially the suffering of the upright, in relation to God's Providence: God's wisdom guides all events, and misfortune is no sign of God's hatred but often of his special love. Haydock puts it that the book shows why the wicked sometimes prosper while the good are afflicted.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Job”, VI. Design of the Book↗Haydock (1859), Preface↗

Key themes

  • Suffering of the innocent and the mystery of God's Providence.
  • Patience under trial (James 5:11).
  • The limits of human wisdom before God's works in creation (38-41).
  • Hope beyond death: 'I know that my Redeemer liveth' (19:25).
  • Intercession: God forgives Job's friends at his prayer (42).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Job”, VI. Design of the Book↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Job”, VII. Teaching as to the Future Life↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Job”, IV. Contents↗James 5:11

Structure

The 1913 article finds a prose prologue (1-2), in which Job is tried by Satan with God's permission; a long poetic body (3:1-42:6) of speeches by Job, his three friends, Eliu and finally God; and a prose epilogue (42:7-17), in which God vindicates Job and restores him double. Challoner notes that in Hebrew the whole of 3-42 is in verse.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Job”, IV. Contents↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Job”, V. Arrangement of the Main, Poetic Portion of the Book↗Challoner, Introduction↗

How the Church reads it

The 1913 article notes that the Latin Martyrology commemorates Job on 10 May and the Greek on 6 May, that the Fathers held him a historical person, and that Christian tradition reads him as a type of Christ. It defends the reading of 19:23-27 as a declaration of the resurrection of the body, the view of all early commentators. It also explains how far the speeches carry divine authority: what the author or God approves is guaranteed, while the friends' words count only where they are approved.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Job”, II. Authority↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Job”, VI. Design of the Book↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Job”, VII. Teaching as to the Future Life↗

In the canon

A protocanonical book, in Trent's list (Session IV) and the Catechism's (CCC 120). The 1913 article notes that the Hebrew Bible places it with Psalms and Proverbs among the Writings, while the Greek and the Vulgate put it after the historical books and before the Psalms.

The old texts and the sources

Haydock's preface (1859 text)
This Book takes its name from the holy man, of whom it treats; who, according to the more probable opinion, was of the race of Esau, and the same as Jobab, king of Edom, mentioned Gen. xxxvi. 33. It is uncertain who was the writer of it. Some attribute it to Job himself; others to Moses, or some one of the prophets. In the Hebrew it is written in verse, from the beginning of the third chapter to the forty-second chapter. (Challoner) — The beginning and conclusion are historical, and in prose. Some have divided this work into a kind of tragedy, the first act extending to C. xv., the second to C. xxii., the third to C. xxxviii., where God appears, and the plot is unfolded. They suppose that the sentiments of the speakers are expressed, though not their own words. This may be very probable: but the opinion of those who look upon the work as a mere allegory, must be rejected with horror. The sacred writers speak of Job as of a personage who had really existed, (Calmet) and set the most noble pattern of virtue, and particularly of patience. Tob. ii. 12. Ezec. xiv. 14. Jam. v. 11. Philo and Josephus pass over this history, as they do those of Tobias, Judith, &c. (Haydock) — The time when Job lived is not clearly ascertained. Some have supposed (Calmet) that he was a contemporary with Esther; (D. Thalmud) on which supposition, the work is here placed in its chronological order. But Job more probably live during the period when the Hebrews groaned under the Egyptian bondage, (Haydock) or sojourned in the wilderness. Num. xiv. 9. The Syrians place the book at the head of the Scriptures. (Calmet) — Its situation has often varied, and is of no great importance. The subject which is here treated, is of far more; as it is intended to shew that the wicked sometimes prosper, while the good are afflicted. (Haydock) — This had seldom been witnessed before the days of Abraham: but as God had now selected his family to be witnesses and guardians of religion, a new order of things was beginning to appear. This greatly perplexed Job himself; who, therefore, confesses that he had not sufficiently understood the ways of God, till he had deigned to explain them in the parable of the two great beasts. C. xlii. 3. We cannot condemn the sentiments expressed by Job, since God has declared that they were right, (ib. v. 8) and reprimands Elihu, (C. xxxviii. 2.) and the other three friends of Job, for maintaining a false opinion, though, from the history of past times, they had judge it to be true. This remark may excupate them from the stain of wilful lying, and vain declamation. (Houbigant) — However, as they assert what was false, their words of themselves are of no authority; and they are even considered as the forerunners of heretics. S. Greg. S. Aug. &c. (Tirinus) — Job refutes them by sound logic. (St. Jerome) — We may discover in this book the sum of Christian morality, (Worthington) for which purpose it has been chiefly explained by S. Gregory. The style is very poetical, (Haydock) though at the same time simple, like that of Moses. (Du Hamel) — It is interspersed with many Arabic and Chaldaic idioms; (St. Jerome) whence some have concluded, that it was written originally by Job and his friends (Haydock) in Arabic, and translated into Hebrew by Moses, for the consolation of his brethren. (Worthington) — The Hebrew text is in many places incorrect; (Houbigant) and the Septuagint seem to have omitted several verses. Orig. — S. Jerom says almost eight hundred, (Calmet) each consisting of about six words. (Haydock) — Shultens, in 1747, expressed his dissatisfaction with the labours of all preceding commentators. To explain this book may not therefore be an easy task: but we must be as short as possible. (Haydock) — Those who desire farther information, may consult Pineda, (Worthington) whose voluminous work, in two folios, will nearly (Haydock) give all necessary information. (Calmet)
