2 Corinthians

At a glance

Author

Paul, with Timothy named in the greeting (1:1). The 1913 article says Paul's authorship is universally acknowledged; only the letter's unity is debated.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles to the Corinthians”, The Second Epistle: Unity↗

Date

A few months after the first letter. Haydock puts it in 57, some months before Romans; Challoner says the same year as the first letter.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles to the Corinthians”, The Second Epistle↗Haydock (1859)↗Challoner↗

Setting

Macedonia, perhaps Philippi, after Paul left Ephesus following the riot stirred up by Demetrius and met Titus coming back from Corinth (7:5-7).

About this book

Audience

The church at Corinth, together with all the faithful in Achaia (1:1).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles to the Corinthians”, The Second Epistle↗

Purpose

Titus brought mostly good news: the Corinthians had repented, and the offender had done penance. Paul writes to comfort them, to explain why he changed his travel plans, to have the penitent restored, to urge the collection for Jerusalem, and to defend his apostleship against false apostles.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles to the Corinthians”, The Second Epistle↗Challoner↗Haydock (1859)↗

Key themes

  • God's comfort in affliction (1)
  • Forgiving and restoring the repentant sinner (2:5-11)
  • The glory of the ministry of the New Covenant and of reconciliation (3; 5:18-20)
  • Generous giving, after the example of Christ who became poor for us (8-9)
  • Strength made perfect in weakness (12:9)
  • Paul's defense of his apostleship (10-13)

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles to the Corinthians”, The Second Epistle: Divisions↗Challoner↗

Structure

Three parts: Paul's conduct and the dignity of his ministry (1-7); the collection for the poor of Jerusalem (8-9); and his defense against the false apostles (10-13).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles to the Corinthians”, The Second Epistle: Divisions of the Epistle↗

How the Church reads it

Challoner reads the letter as teaching true penance and the dignity of New Testament ministers, since Paul absolves the man he had earlier excluded. The Catechism draws on 5:18-20 for the ministry of reconciliation entrusted to the apostles and their successors (CCC 1424, 1442), on 5:21 for Christ made sin for our sake (CCC 602), and on 12:9 for grace sufficient in sickness and weakness (CCC 1508).

In the canon

Protocanonical; it has been received as Paul's from the earliest lists.

The old texts and the sources

Haydock's preface (1859 text)
The subject and design of this second Epistle to the Corinthians, is much the same as of the former. He comforts and congratulates with those who were now reformed by his admonitions. He blames the faulty with apostolical liberty; and being forced to justify himself and his proceedings against the upstart false teachers, he gives an ample account of his sufferings, and also of the favours and graces, which God had bestowed upon him. This Epistle was written not long after the first, (an. 57.) some months before that to the Romans, from some place in Macedonia, perhaps from Philippi, as marked at the end of divers Greek copies, though it is observed, that those subscriptions are not much to be relied upon. (Witham) — In this Epistle S. Paul comforts those who are now reformed by his admonitions to them in the former, and absolves the incestuous man on doing penance, whom he had before excommunicated for his crime. Hence he treats of true penance, and of the dignity of the ministers of the New Testament. He cautions the faithful against false teachers, and the society of infidels. He gives an account of his sufferings, and also of the favours and graces which God hath bestowed on him. (Challoner) — S. Paul, not being able to come to the Corinthians as soon as he had promised, writes this Epistle to inform them, that it was not through inconstancy, but on account of several weighty reasons, which had hitherto hindered him. Several other reasons, likewise, compelled him to write. For during his absence, several false teachers of the Jews had come amongst them, teaching them that it was necessary to observe the law of Moses, in order to be saved. S. Paul, therefore, first excuses himself, by saying, that the afflictions and troubles he had met with, had hindered him from coming to them. He next orders the fornicator to be restored to favour; after which, he extols his apostleship, forming a comparison between the law of Christ, and of Moses, wherein he blames the false teachers. He then subjoins an exhortation to a pious and holy life, with liberality in their alms, after the example of the Macedonians. As the false teachers had been very industrious in establishing their own reputation, by detracting from that of S. Paul, he enumerates his own sufferings, and the favours he had received from God, shewing that he had much more reason to glory than they; and concludes by exhorting them to correct those faults with which they still remained infected. (Estius) — This letter may be justly appreciated as a perfect masterpiece of that animated and solid eloquence, which all interpreters so much admire in S. Paul. (Bible de Vence)
