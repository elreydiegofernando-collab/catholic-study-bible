Esther

16 chapters

At a glance

Author

The 1913 article says there is nothing but conjecture: the Talmud names the Great Synagogue, St. Clement of Alexandria names Mardochai, and St. Augustine suggests Esdras. Challoner reports that almost all commentators make Mardochai the writer, from 9:20, and Haydock (citing Calmet) thinks the Greek translator Lysimachus probably wrote the additions.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Esther”, Book of Esther↗Challoner, Introduction↗Haydock (1859), Preface↗

Date

The 1913 article places the story under Assuerus, whom it identifies with Xerxes I (485-465 B.C.), and notes that the book may have been written in Persia late in his reign or under Artaxerxes I (465-425 B.C.). Most modern critics, it reports, date it to the early Greek period after 332 B.C., some as late as 135 B.C.

Setting

Susa, the Persian capital, at the court of King Assuerus, among the Jews of the Dispersion whose families had been deported from Jerusalem.

About this book

Audience

The Jews who keep the feast of Purim: the 1913 article notes that the book was read in the synagogues at that feast, and Haydock that the Jews hold it in the highest regard.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Esther”, Book of Esther↗Haydock (1859), Preface↗

Purpose

It tells how Esther and Mardochai, by courage, fasting and prayer, saved the Jews of the Persian empire from Aman's plot, and why the feast of Purim ('lots') is kept. The 1913 article answers critics who call the book irreligious that they read only the Hebrew part; with the Greek parts the whole is plainly religious.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Esther”↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Esther”, Book of Esther↗

Key themes

  • Providence: God works through events even where the Hebrew text never names him.
  • Fasting and prayer before a great risk.
  • An intercessor who goes before the king for her people.
  • The proud brought low (Aman) and the humble raised (Mardochai).
  • The feast of Purim as a memorial of deliverance.

Structure

The 1913 article, reading the chapters in time order, divides the story into two parts: the events that led to the decree against the Jews, and the Jews' escape and triumph; Haydock gives the full order for reading. In the Vulgate itself, chapters 1:1-10:3 follow the Hebrew and 10:4-16:24 hold the Greek passages.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Esther”, Book of Esther↗Haydock (1859), Preface↗

How the Church reads it

The 1913 article states that the Fathers of the Church considered Esther a type of the Blessed Virgin Mary. The Catechism names Esther with Judith among the holy women who kept alive Israel's hope of salvation (CCC 64) and whose mission prepared for Mary's (CCC 489).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Esther”↗CCC 64↗CCC 489↗

In the canon

The Hebrew part is protocanonical; the Greek parts are deuterocanonical. The 1913 article notes that when some of Luther's followers rejected the Greek parts, the Council of Trent declared them canonical and inspired: its canon receives the listed books 'entire with all their parts' as found in the Vulgate (Session IV; CCC 120).

The old texts and the sources

Haydock's preface (1859 text)
This Book takes its name from queen Esther; whose history is here recorded. The general opinion of almost all commentators on the Holy Scripture, make Mardochai the writer of it: which also may be collected below from C. ix. 20. (Challoner) — He and the queen were certainly authors of the letter, (Haydock) enjoining the celebration of the feast of Purim, or "lots," which is the ground-work (Calmet) of the present narration. (Du Hamel) — The compiler has also had recourse to the archives of the kingdom of Persia: so that his work has all the authority that can be required of a profane historian; and being moreover inspired in all its parts, we cannot refuse to receive it with the utmost respect. Those additions which are not now in Hebrew, (Calmet) though they were perhaps formerly, (Worthington; Origen; Du Hamel) have been carefully preserved by S. Jerom, and were recognized by the ancient Vulg. as they are at present by the Greek, without any distinction. Lysimachus, the Greek translator, was probably the author of them. C. xi. 1. (Calmet) — The objections of Capellus against this "Greek scribbler," as he is pleased to style him, despising the judgment of both Jews and Christians, are in general very unaccountably borrowed (Haydock) from the Latin version, and are easily refuted. (Houbigant) — Those Jews, who have rejected this work entirely, with Melito, (Eus. Hist. iv. 26. S. Greg. Naz. &c.) ought not to prevail against the consent of the majority, (Calmet) expressed in the Councils of Laodicea, Carthage, Trent, sess. 4, &c. To read this book according to the order of time, we should begin C. xi. v. 2, &c. C. i. ii. and xii. and iii. to v. 14; then we find the distress of the Jews in the rest of that chapter, and in C. xiii. to v. 8, and their delivery in C. iv. to ix. v. 17, and C. xiii. v. 8, &c. and C. xiv. xv. and xvi. The consequences of these events are recorded C. ix. v. 17, &c. to C. xi. 1. (Worthington) with which verse the book ends, in the Greek editions. (Haydock) — They vary considerably, as did the copies of the ancient Vulgate, which called forth the complaints of S. Jerom, Pref. But the Church has distinguished what was spurious from the genuine word of God; so that the doubts of Lyran, Sixtus, (Bib. viii.) &c. respecting the fragments at the end of the book being not canonical, ought no longer to be indulged; much less can the boldness of many Lutherans, (Calmet) and particularly of Le Clerc, (Houbigant) be tolerated, who represent the whole work as a mere fiction. The Jews have a greater respect for it than for any of the prophets; whose works, they say, will perish at the coming of the Messias: whereas this will subsist with the books of Moses, and the feast of Purim will never be abolished. C. ix. 28. Maimon. — Ben. Gorion (ii. 2.) admits the additions. But Josephus is silent about them, as he probably did not find them in his copy. (Calmet) — He recites, however, both the epistles of Assuerus. Ant. xi. 6. (Du Hamel) — It is not agreed whether these events happened before or after the captivity. But it is now most commonly supposed, that Esther was married to Darius Hystaspes, A. 3489, about the time of the dedication of the temple. C. xiv. 9. He had been on the throne six years, and reigned other thirty. See Herod. vii. 4. (Calmet) — Josephus thinks that Esther was the queen of Artaxerxes Longimanus, who was a great friend of the Jews. (Du Hamel) — The Thalmud attributes this work to the great Synagogue, consisting of Esdras, Mardochai, Joachim, &c. and, as various persons might write the same history, the Greek, with the additions, seems to be taken from one copy, and the Hebrew from another rather more concise, (Huet. D.) but equally inspired. (Haydock)
