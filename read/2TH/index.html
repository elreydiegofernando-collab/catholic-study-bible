2 Thessalonians

3 chapters

At a glance

Author

Paul, with Silvanus and Timothy (1:1), who signs the greeting in his own hand as the mark of his letters (3:17). The 1913 article cites Polycarp, Ignatius, Justin and Marcion for its early use as Paul's.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles to the Thessalonians”, III.A Authenticity↗Challoner↗

Date

Soon after the first letter, with Silas and Timothy still beside Paul. Haydock gives about 52 and Challoner the same year as the first; the 1913 article's two chronologies put both letters in 48-49 or 53-54.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles to the Thessalonians”, III.C Time and Place; II.C↗Haydock (1859)↗Challoner↗

Setting

Corinth, during Paul's year and a half there (Acts 18).

About this book

Audience

The church at Thessalonica, still under persecution.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles to the Thessalonians”, III.E Contents↗

Purpose

Some at Thessalonica had been alarmed, perhaps by a forged letter in Paul's name, into thinking the day of the Lord had already come (2:2), and some had stopped working. Paul writes that certain signs must come first, rebukes the idle, and tells them to hold fast to the traditions they were taught, by word or by letter.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles to the Thessalonians”, III.D Occasion↗Challoner↗Haydock (1859)↗

Key themes

  • God's just judgment, and comfort for the persecuted (1)
  • The day of the Lord is not yet: first the revolt and the man of sin (2:1-12, Douay 2:1-11)
  • What now restrains him until his time (2:6-7)
  • Hold the traditions received by word or by letter (Vulgate and modern 2:15, Douay 2:14)
  • Work quietly; whoever will not work, let him not eat (3:6-12)

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles to the Thessalonians”, III.E Contents↗Challoner↗

Structure

Chapter 1: greeting, thanksgiving for their faith and love, and assurance of God's justice; chapter 2: the signs that must come before the Lord's coming; chapter 3: requests for prayer, a charge to avoid the disorderly, Paul's example of work, and his autograph greeting.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles to the Thessalonians”, III.E Contents↗

How the Church reads it

The Catechism reads 2:4-12 of the Church's final trial and the 'mystery of iniquity' before Christ returns (CCC 671, 675), and 3:6-13 of the duty to work and trust in providence (CCC 2427, 2830). Challoner and Haydock point to the charge to hold the traditions 'whether by word, or by epistle' (Douay 2:14) as the apostolic command to keep both oral and written teaching.

In the canon

Protocanonical; listed with 1 Thessalonians among the undisputed books from the earliest lists.

Historical notes

Historical note18 June 1915 · Pontifical Biblical Commission

De parousia in epistolis Sancti Pauli Apostoli (On the Parousia in the letters of St. Paul)

AAS 7 (1915) 357-358

Answered that a Catholic interpreter may not say the apostles voiced mistaken human views about the time of the Lord's coming, and that Paul wrote nothing out of keeping with the ignorance of that time which Christ declared. Its third answer concerns 1 Thessalonians 4:15-17.

Pontifical Biblical Commission (1915)↗Vatican II, Dei Verbum, 11↗

The Pontifical Biblical Commission's answers of 1905-1915 bound Catholics when they were issued (Pius X, Praestantia Scripturae, 1907). In 1955 the Commission's secretary and under-secretary wrote, in an unofficial statement, that scholars are free on such points except where they touch faith and morals. Since 1971 the Commission has been an advisory body. These notes record what was decided and when; they are not badges.

The old texts and the sources

Haydock's preface (1859 text)
In this epistle S. Paul admonishes the Thessalonians to be constant in the faith of Christ, and not to be terrified by the insinuations of false teachers, telling them that the day of judgment was near at hand, as there must come many signs and wonders before it. He bids them to hold firm the traditions received from him, whether by word or by epistle; and shews them how they may be certain of his letters by the manner he writes. This epistle was written soon after the former, and also from Corinth, about A. 52. (Challoner; Witham) — From the context we learn that the present is a continuation of the former epistle. He not only rectifies wrong impressions caused by his former letter, but finding that those indolent characters whom he had reprimanded were no ways corrected, he determines to reprimand them still more severely in this letter, which he tells us he writes because he has it not in his power to visit, as he could wish, the Thessalonians.
