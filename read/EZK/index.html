Ezekiel

Douay: Ezechiel · 48 chapters

At a glance

Author

Ezechiel son of Buzi, a priest deported to Babylon with King Joachin in 598 B.C., who settled at Tell-Abib by the river Chobar; Challoner adds that he is said to have died a martyr. The 1913 article says the genuineness of the book is generally conceded.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Ezechiel”↗Challoner, Introduction↗

Date

He was called in the fifth year of Joachin's captivity (about 593 B.C.) and prophesied for at least twenty-two years (29:17).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Ezechiel”↗

Setting

Among the exiles in Babylonia, while Jerusalem still stood and then after its fall; he prophesied in Babylon, Challoner says, to the same effect as Jeremias in Jerusalem.

About this book

Audience

The captives, who, Haydock says, had begun to regret listening to Jeremias's counsel to submit to Babylon; first warned that Jerusalem would fall, then comforted with the promise of return.

Haydock (1859), Preface↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Ezechiel”↗

Purpose

The 1913 article says he was called to proclaim God's faithfulness in trials and in his promises: first the certain destruction of Juda, city and Temple, then the return from exile and redemption in the kingdom of the Messias, the second David, so that the people would not despair.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Ezechiel”↗

Key themes

  • The vision of God's glory on the chariot-throne (1).
  • Personal responsibility: the soul that sins shall die (18).
  • The Good Shepherd who seeks his scattered sheep (34).
  • A new heart and a new spirit; the dry bones raised (36-37).
  • The new Temple, with water flowing from it (40-48).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Ezechiel”↗CCC 754↗CCC 1287↗CCC 715↗

Structure

After the call vision (1-3:21), the 1913 article finds three parts: prophecies against Juda before the fall of Jerusalem (3:22-24), oracles against the nations (25-32), and prophecies of restoration after the fall, ending with the vision of the new Temple and land (33-48).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Ezechiel”↗

How the Church reads it

The Catechism hears in God's promise to give a new heart and put his spirit within his people the announcement of the Spirit's outpouring (CCC 1287, 715), and in the shepherd who gathers his flock the image of Christ the Good Shepherd and his Church (CCC 754). The 1913 article notes that the New Testament, though it never quotes him word for word, often borrows his figures (compare John 10 with Ezechiel 34), and that St. John in the Apocalypse often follows him.

CCC 1287↗CCC 715↗CCC 754↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Ezechiel”↗

In the canon

A protocanonical book (Trent, Session IV; CCC 120). Haydock notes that some Jews hesitated over it because its laws and temple seemed to differ from Moses and from Solomon's temple, and the 1913 article that Sirach already recognized its divine character (Ecclesiasticus 49:10-11 Douay).

The old texts and the sources

Haydock's preface (1859 text)
Ezechiel, whose name signifies the strength of God, was of the priestly race, and of the number of the captives that were carried away to Babylon with king Joachin. He was contemporary with Jeremias, and prophesied to the same effect in Babylon as Jeremias did in Jerusalem; and is said to have ended his days in like manner, by martyrdom. (Challoner) — He strove to comfort the captives, who began to repine that they had listened too readily to Jeremias, exhorting them to submit to the king of Babylon. Some think that part of his prophecies is lost, as Josephus mentions two books: but the nine last chapters, regarding the new city and temple, might form the second division. The Jews hesitated whether to allow his works to be canonical, as they seemed to differ from Moses, and from the dimensions given of Solomon's temple. But the same God might surely suggest some improvements, and the morality of the prophet is most excellent. (Calmet) — His style may be compared to that of Homer (Grotius) and Alcæus. Many have thought that (Calmet) Pythagoras was his disciple; (Eus. præp. xiii.) yet the latter seems to have lived after the prophet, who was led into captivity with Jechonias, A. 3410, and prophesied for twenty years. He dates from this period, (Calmet) and from the renewal of the covenant under Josias, (C. i. 1.; Haydock) when the captivity was first announced. (Worthington) — The Jews allowed none to read the first and the nine (Haydock) last chapters, nor the beginning of Gen. nor the Cant. before they were thirty years old; and they never attempted to explain the vision nor the building of the temple, supposing it to be above the power of man. St. Jerom.
