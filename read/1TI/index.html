1 Timothy

6 chapters

At a glance

Author

Paul (1:1). The 1913 article stresses that no Catholic doubted this in early times: Eusebius lists the three Pastoral letters among the books received everywhere, and Polycarp, Ignatius, Irenaeus and Tertullian knew them as Paul's.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles to Timothy and Titus”, II. External Evidence↗Challoner↗Haydock (1859)↗

Date

After Paul's release from his first Roman imprisonment. The 1913 article places 1 Timothy and Titus in 63-67, when he traveled again in the East; Challoner and Haydock give about thirty-three years after the Ascension.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles to Timothy and Titus”, I.F Objection: No room for them in the life-time of St. Paul↗Challoner↗Haydock (1859)↗

Setting

Uncertain; the more general opinion, in Challoner and Haydock, is Macedonia, where Paul was going when he left Timothy at Ephesus (1:3).

About this book

Audience

Timothy, Paul's 'beloved son' and companion, whose father was a Gentile and whom Paul had taken on at Lystra (Acts 16:1-3). Challoner and Haydock call him bishop of Ephesus, where tradition says he died.

Haydock (1859)↗Challoner↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles to Timothy and Titus”, Sts. Timothy and Titus↗

Purpose

To instruct Timothy in governing the church: guarding sound doctrine against false teachers, ordering public prayer, choosing bishops and deacons with care ('impose not hands lightly upon any man', 5:22), and caring for widows, presbyters and the rich. Haydock notes that Augustine wanted all who serve the Church to keep these letters before their eyes.

Challoner↗Haydock (1859)↗

Key themes

  • Guarding the deposit of sound doctrine (1:3-11; 6:20)
  • Prayer for all, since God wills all to be saved and there is one mediator, Christ Jesus (2:1-6)
  • The qualities required of bishops and deacons (3:1-13)
  • The Church, the pillar and ground of the truth, and the great mystery of godliness (3:15-16)
  • The grace given by the laying on of hands (4:14)
  • Widows, presbyters, slaves and the danger of riches (5-6)

Challoner↗Haydock (1859)↗

Structure

Greeting and charge against false teachers (1); public prayer and order in the assembly (2); bishops and deacons, and the mystery of godliness (3); Timothy's own conduct against coming errors (4); duties toward widows, presbyters and others (5); slaves, false teachers, riches and a final charge (6).

Douay-Rheims text, the letter's chapters↗Haydock (1859)↗

How the Church reads it

The Catechism draws on 2:4-5 for God's will that all be saved and for Christ as the one mediator, which the saints' intercession serves rather than rivals (CCC 74, 480, 618, 956), and on 3:15 for the Church as 'pillar and bulwark of the truth' that guards the faith (CCC 171, 2032). It reads 3:1-13 and 4:14 of ordination by the laying on of hands (CCC 1577, 1590) and counts 3:16 among the early hymns to Christ (CCC 463, 2641).

In the canon

Protocanonical; Eusebius counted the Pastoral letters among the books received everywhere, though Marcion rejected them.

Historical notes

Historical note12 June 1913 · Pontifical Biblical Commission

De auctore, de integritate et de compositionis tempore Epistolarum Pastoralium Pauli Apostoli (On the author, integrity and date of the Pastoral Letters)

AAS 5 (1913) 292-293

Held that the two letters to Timothy and the letter to Titus must be held certainly written by Paul and always counted genuine and canonical, and that the 'fragment hypothesis' cannot weaken that tradition. It held that objections from style, from the errors described and from the developed church order do not weaken their genuineness, and that, Paul's two Roman captivities being certain, they were written between his release and his death.

Pontifical Biblical Commission (1913)↗

The Pontifical Biblical Commission's answers of 1905-1915 bound Catholics when they were issued (Pius X, Praestantia Scripturae, 1907). In 1955 the Commission's secretary and under-secretary wrote, in an unofficial statement, that scholars are free on such points except where they touch faith and morals. Since 1971 the Commission has been an advisory body. These notes record what was decided and when; they are not badges.

The old texts and the sources

Haydock's preface (1859 text)
S. Paul passing through Lycaonia, about the year 51, some of the brethren at Derbe or Lystra recommended to him a disciple, by name Timothy, who from his infancy had studied the Holy Scriptures. S. Paul took him, making him his companion and fellow-labourer in the gospel: and not to offend the Jews, who could not be ignorant that Timothy's father was a Gentile, he caused him to be circumcised. Afterwards he ordained him bishop of Ephesus. (Witham) — S. Paul writes this epistle to his beloved Timothy, to instruct him in the duties of a bishop, both in respect to himself and to his charge; and that he ought to be well informed of the good morals of those on whom he was to impose hands: Impose not hands lightly upon any man. He tells him also how he should behave towards his clergy. This epistle was written about thirty-three years after our Lord's ascension; but where it was written is uncertain: the more general opinion is, that it was in Macedonia. (Challoner) — After his epistles to the Churches, now follow those to particular persons; to Timothy and Titus, who were bishops, and to Philemon. Timothy was the beloved disciple of S. Paul, whom he frequently styles his son; but it is not certain that they were at all related. After having accompanied the apostle in many of his travels, the latter at last ordained him bishop, and fixed him permanently at Ephesus. Shortly after he wrote him this epistle, to instruct him in the episcopal duties, as he was but young for those great functions. He might be then about thirty-five. He mentions, likewise, in short the chief heresies which were then making mischief at Ephesus, and gives regulations and instructions for different states of persons in the Church. S. Timothy, who had been so long the disciple of S. Paul, and who never left him except when ordered by his master, could not be ignorant of his duties, but it was destined for the use of bishops of every age. Hence S. Austin says that such as are destined to serve the Church, should have continually before their eyes the two epistles to Timothy and that to Titus.
Challoner's introduction (1749–52)
St. Paul write this Epistle to his BELOVED TIMOTHY, being then bishop of Ephesus, to instruct him in the duties of a bishop, both in respect to himself and to his charge; and that he ought to be well informed of the good morals of those on whom he was to impose hands: Impose not hands lightly upon any man. He tells him also how he should behave towards his clergy. The Epistle was written about 33 years after our Lord’s Ascension; but where it was written is uncertain: the more general opinion is, that it was in Macedonia.
