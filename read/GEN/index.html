Genesis

At a glance

Author

Jewish and Christian tradition names Moses as the author of Genesis and the rest of the Pentateuch, and the 1913 article defends that view at length. It also notes that Catholic tradition never required Moses to have written every word himself: in the 16th century Cardinal Bellarmine held that Esdras gathered, corrected and completed the books. Haydock treats Moses as the author of all five books.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Pentateuch”, III. Authenticity, B. Witness of Tradition, (2) Christian Tradition↗Haydock (1859), Preface↗

Date

Tradition dates Genesis to the lifetime of Moses: the 15th century B.C. on Usher's chronology, which Haydock follows, or the 13th century if Israel's captivity fell under Ramses II, as a scholar cited in the 1913 article held. The 1906 decree still allowed later inspired additions, and the critics the article answers put the final form after the Exile, in the 5th century B.C.

Setting

Genesis runs from the creation of the world to the death of Joseph in Egypt, a span Challoner counts as 2,369 years. Its first eleven chapters concern all mankind; from chapter 12 it follows Abraham's family from Mesopotamia into Chanaan and finally down to Egypt.

About this book

Audience

It was written first for the people of Israel, as the opening of the Law of Moses: it told them who made the world, how sin entered it, and how God chose their fathers. The Catechism notes that the inspired authors set the creation chapters at the head of Scripture to teach these truths to every reader.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Pentateuch”, II. Analysis, A. Genesis↗CCC 289↗

Purpose

The 1913 article says Genesis prepares the reader for the Law: it tells how God chose one family to keep his revelation and trained it for its mission. Haydock adds that it teaches creation out of nothing against pagan myth and the eternity of the world, and shows God's providence over his chosen people down to the death of Joseph.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Pentateuch”, II. Analysis, A. Genesis↗Haydock (1859), Preface↗

Key themes

  • God creates all things, freely and good, out of nothing.
  • Man and woman made in God's image, and the first sin with its consequences.
  • The first promise of a Redeemer after the fall (3:15, the 'Protoevangelium').
  • God's covenant promises to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, and Abraham's faith.
  • Providence that brings good out of evil, above all in the story of Joseph.

Haydock (1859), Preface↗CCC 296↗CCC 390↗CCC 410↗CCC 145–146↗CCC 312↗

Structure

The 1913 article divides Genesis into two unequal parts: chapters 1-11, a general history of mankind, and chapters 12-50, the history of the chosen family. Each part has five sections, each opening with the Hebrew formula toledoth, 'these are the generations', which the article reads as 'this is the history of'. In each pair the side branch comes first and briefly (Cain before Seth, Ismael before Isaac, Esau before Jacob), then the chosen line.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Pentateuch”, II. Analysis, A. Genesis↗

How the Church reads it

The Catechism gives the first three chapters a special place among the Bible's texts on creation: in solemn language they teach creation's origin and goal in God, its order and goodness, man's calling and the tragedy of sin (CCC 289). It describes the six days as a symbolic presentation of God's work (CCC 337), says Genesis 3 uses figurative language while affirming a real first sin at the start of human history (CCC 390), and calls 3:15 the 'Protoevangelium', the first announcement of the Redeemer (CCC 410). It also holds up Abraham as the model of the obedience of faith (CCC 145-146).

In the canon

Genesis heads the 'five books of Moses' in the Council of Trent's list of the canonical Scriptures, and it stands first in the Catechism's list of the canon (CCC 120).

Historical notes

Historical note27 June 1906 · Pontifical Biblical Commission

On the Mosaic authenticity of the Pentateuch (De mosaica authentia Pentateuchi)

ASS 39 (1906) 377

The Commission answered that the critics' arguments did not justify denying that Moses is the author of the Pentateuch. It allowed that he used written or oral sources, that he may have had others write the work under his direction and then approved it, and that the text later received inspired additions, updated wording and copyists' errors.

Historical note30 June 1909 · Pontifical Biblical Commission

On the historical character of the first three chapters of Genesis (De charactere historico trium priorum capitum Geneseos)

AAS 1 (1909) 567-569

The Commission held that Genesis 1-3 is not myth, mere allegory or legend, and that its literal, historical sense must be kept where it touches the foundations of the faith: creation by God at the beginning of time, the special creation of man, the first woman formed from the first man, the unity of the human race, the first parents' original state, the command and the fall, and the promise of a Redeemer. It also allowed differing opinions where the Fathers differ, said Genesis 1 is not written in scientific language, and allowed 'day' to mean a natural day or a period of time.

Its safeguards on creation by God, the special creation of man, the unity of the human race and a real first sin match current teaching (CCC 290, 355-361, 390).

Historical note16 January 1948 · Pontifical Biblical Commission

Letter to Cardinal Suhard on the sources of the Pentateuch and the literary form of Genesis 1-11

AAS 40 (1948) 45-48

In a letter to the Archbishop of Paris, the Commission said scholars no longer doubt that the Pentateuch drew on sources and that its laws grew over time, and it invited Catholic scholars to study these questions freely. It added that Genesis 1-11 does not fit classical or modern categories of history-writing, yet relates in simple, figurative language the basic truths presupposed by salvation and a popular account of human origins.

Pontifical Biblical Commission (1948)↗

The Pontifical Biblical Commission's answers of 1905-1915 bound Catholics when they were issued (Pius X, Praestantia Scripturae, 1907). In 1955 the Commission's secretary and under-secretary wrote, in an unofficial statement, that scholars are free on such points except where they touch faith and morals. Since 1971 the Commission has been an advisory body. These notes record what was decided and when; they are not badges.
