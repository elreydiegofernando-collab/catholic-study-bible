Douay: Isaias · 66 chapters

At a glance

Author

Isaias son of Amos, of Jerusalem, who prophesied for about half a century under Ozias, Joatham, Achaz and Ezechias; Challoner adds the Hebrew tradition that he was of royal blood and was sawn in two under Manasses. The 1913 article reports that critics assign chapters 40-66 to a 'Second Isaias' among the exiles, and sets against them the 1908 decree of the Biblical Commission, which upheld one author.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Isaias”, I. Life↗Challoner, Introduction↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Isaias”, II. The Book of Isaias, Second Isaias↗

Date

Isaias's ministry ran from about the death of King Ozias (about 740 B.C.) into the reign of Ezechias, near 700 B.C.; Haydock says he began ten years before the founding of Rome. Critics who divide the book date chapters 40-66 to the late Exile, about 540 B.C., and parts later still.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Isaias”, I. Life↗Haydock (1859), Preface↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Isaias”, II. The Book of Isaias, Second Isaias↗

Setting

Jerusalem and Juda during the Assyrian crisis: the Syro-Ephraimite war under Achaz, the fall of Samaria, and Sennacherib's siege of Jerusalem under Ezechias; chapters 40-66 speak to Israel in the Babylonian exile.

About this book

Audience

The kings and people of Juda in Isaias's own day, and, in the second collection, Israel mourning in exile; the 1908 decree, as the 1913 article reports it, denies that a prophet must always address only his contemporaries.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Isaias”, II. The Book of Isaias, Second Isaias↗

Purpose

To call Juda to trust in God alone rather than in foreign alliances, to announce judgment and a faithful remnant, and to proclaim the coming deliverance, the Messias and the Servant of the Lord. Challoner says he foretold the coming of Christ, the mysteries of our redemption and the calling of the Gentiles so clearly that he seems rather an evangelist than a prophet.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Isaias”, III. Appreciation of the Work of Isaias↗Challoner, Introduction↗

Key themes

  • The holiness of God, 'the Holy One of Israel' (6).
  • Emmanuel, the child born of the virgin (7:14), and the shoot of Jesse (11).
  • Faith in God rather than in alliances with the nations.
  • The Servant of the Lord who suffers for the many (52:13-53:12).
  • Comfort, return and the new Jerusalem open to all nations (40; 60-66).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Isaias”, II. The Book of Isaias↗CCC 712↗CCC 713↗CCC 601↗

Structure

The 1913 article finds two collections of discourses: chapters 1-35 (often called 'First Isaias') and 40-66 ('Second Isaias' to many modern critics), with a stretch of historical narrative between them (36-39). Within the second it follows Condamin's division: Cyrus and the deliverance (40-48), the Servant songs and the new Jerusalem (49-55, 60-62, with 42:1-7), and the closing exhortations (56-66).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Isaias”, II. The Book of Isaias↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Isaias”, II. The Book of Isaias, Second Isaias↗

How the Church reads it

The Catechism finds the Messias's features in the 'Book of Emmanuel' and the shoot of Jesse (CCC 712), reads the Servant songs as proclaiming the meaning of Jesus' Passion (CCC 713), and sees Christ's redeeming death fulfill the prophecy of the suffering Servant (CCC 601). The 1913 article notes that the Fathers, echoing Sirach's praise of 'the great prophet' (Ecclesiasticus 48:25 Douay), agree that Isaias was the greatest of the writing prophets, and Haydock quotes St. Jerome that he might seem an evangelist.

In the canon

A protocanonical book, first of the major prophets in Trent's list (Session IV) and in the Catechism's (CCC 120).

Historical notes

Historical note28 June 1908 · Pontifical Biblical Commission

On the character and author of the Book of Isaias (De libri Isaiae indole et auctore)

ASS 41 (1908) 613

Admitting true prophecy, the Commission answered that a prophet may foretell things far in the future and need not always address his contemporaries, so the Exile standpoint of chapters 40-66 does not prove another author. It held that arguments from language and style, even taken together, do not prove several authors for the book.

Its premise that true prophecy of distant events is possible matches Vatican I's teaching on prophecy as a sign of revelation (Dei Filius, ch. 3).

The Pontifical Biblical Commission's answers of 1905-1915 bound Catholics when they were issued (Pius X, Praestantia Scripturae, 1907). In 1955 the Commission's secretary and under-secretary wrote, in an unofficial statement, that scholars are free on such points except where they touch faith and morals. Since 1971 the Commission has been an advisory body. These notes record what was decided and when; they are not badges.

The old texts and the sources

Haydock's preface (1859 text)
This inspired writer is called by the Holy Ghost, (Ecclesiastic. xlviii. 25.) the great prophet; from the greatness of his prophetic spirit, by which he hath foretold, so long before, and in so clear a manner, the coming of Christ, the mysteries of our redemption, the calling of the Gentiles, and the glorious establishment, and perpeutal flourishing of the Church of Christ: insomuch that he seems to have been rather an evangelist than a prophet. His very name is not without mystery: for Isaias in Hebrew signifies the salvation of the Lord, or, Jesus is the Lord. He was, according to the tradition of the Hebrews, of the blood royal of the kings of Juda; an after a most holy life, ended his days by a glorious martyrdom; being sawed in two, at the command of his wicked sonin-law, king Manasses, for reproving his evil ways. (Challoner) — He began to prophesy ten years before the foundation of Rome, and the ruin of Ninive. His style is suitable to his high birth. He may be called the prophet of the mercies of the Lord. Under the figure of the return from captivity, he foretells the redemption of mankind (Calmet) with such perspicuity, that he might seem to be an evangelist. (St. Jerome)
