1 Kings

Douay: 3 Kings · 22 chapters

At a glance

Author

Challoner thinks these books were written by the succession of prophets who recorded the events of their days. The Babylonian Talmud names Jeremias, which the 1913 article finds probable for many reasons of style; Haydock, quoting Calmet, thinks Esdras most probably compiled the prophets' memoirs after the captivity.

Challoner, Introduction↗Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Third and Fourth Books of Kings”↗Haydock (1859), Preface↗

Date

The 1913 article says the books were not finished in their present form before the middle of the Exile, since the last verses tell of Joachin's release in 562 B.C.; if Jeremias wrote them, it was shortly before or after the fall of Jerusalem. Calmet, in Haydock, puts the compiling after the captivity.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Third and Fourth Books of Kings”↗Haydock (1859), Preface↗

Setting

Challoner says these books run from the start of Solomon's reign to the captivity; this book covers Solomon, the temple, the split of the kingdom under Roboam and Jeroboam, and the kings of both kingdoms to Josaphat and Achab, with the prophet Elias.

About this book

Audience

The people of Juda in or after the Exile: the 1913 article says the books explain the fall of both kingdoms and revive hope in God's promise to David.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Third and Fourth Books of Kings”↗

Purpose

The 1913 article says the books do not give a full political history but judge each king by his faithfulness to God's worship, showing that idolatry and the high places brought down Israel and Juda. They also recall God's mercy for David's sake, so that the whole may be called an explanation of Nathan's oracle (2 Kings 7 Douay, 2 Samuel 7 modern).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Third and Fourth Books of Kings”↗

Key themes

  • Solomon's God-given wisdom, his temple and his fall.
  • The dedication of the temple and God's presence in the cloud of glory.
  • The kingdom divided for Solomon's sins; Jeroboam's golden calves.
  • Elias against Achab and the prophets of Baal: 'The Lord he is God' (18:39).
  • Each king judged by fidelity to the one God and his worship.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Third and Fourth Books of Kings”↗CCC 2580↗CCC 697↗CCC 2582–2583↗

Structure

The 1913 article proposes three parts for 3 and 4 Kings together: Solomon (3 Kings 1-11); the two kingdoms side by side to the fall of Israel (3 Kings 12-4 Kings 17); and Juda alone from Ezechias to the destruction of Jerusalem (4 Kings 18-25). This book holds the first part and the start of the second.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Third and Fourth Books of Kings”↗

How the Church reads it

The Catechism takes Solomon's prayer at the dedication of the temple, relying on God's promise and covenant, as a high point of Old Testament prayer (CCC 2580), and counts the cloud that filled it among the Old Testament figures that Christ fulfills in the Holy Spirit (CCC 697). It calls Elias the 'father' of the prophets and draws on his prayer on Carmel and at Horeb (CCC 2582-2583); St. James cites Elias to encourage prayer (James 5:17-18). Haydock, quoting Calmet, says the writer cares more for the house of David, the rewards of piety and the glory of the temple than for military exploits.

In the canon

This is the third of Trent's 'four books of Kings' (Session IV); the Catechism lists it as 1 Kings (CCC 120). The 1913 article notes that 3 and 4 Kings were one book in Hebrew, split later at an awkward place in the middle of Ochozias's reign.

The old texts and the sources

Haydock's preface (1859 text)
This and the following Book are called by the holy Fathers, The Third and Fourth Book of Kings; but by the Hebrews, the First and Second. They contain the history of the kingdoms of Israel and Juda, from the beginning of the reign of Solomon to the captivity. As to the writer of these books, it seem most probable they were not written by one man, nor at one time; but as there was all along a succession of prophets in Israel, who recorded, by divine inspiration, the most remarkable things that happened in their days, these books seem to have been written by these prophets. See 2 Paral. alias 2 Chron. ix. 29, xii. 15, xiii. 22, xx. 34, xxvi. 22, xxxii. 32. (Challoner) — This book informs us of the death of David, C. ii. 11, where some Greek copies concluded the second book "of the reigns or kingdoms," as they style all the four books. Theodoret and Diodorus follow this division. The point is of no consequence; and the Hebrew editions have often varied. Origen observes, that the Jews denoted these tow books from the first words, "Ouammelech David." Euseb. Hist. vi. 25. (Haydock) — In S. Jerom's time, the four books made only two. The present book details the actions of Solomon, (Calmet) till the end of the 12th chapter. Then we behold the division of the kingdom: Roboam, Abias, Asa, and Josaphat, reign over Juda; Jeroboam, &c. over Israel; while the prophets Abias, Elias, Eliseus, appear in the remaining eleven chapters. (Worthington) — Though the memoirs seem to have been left by contemporary authors, (Haydock) one, and most probably Esdras, made the compilation, after the captivity, inserting frequently the very words of his authors, yet so as to make some additional reflections. (Calmet) — The Rabbins generally attribute the work to Jeremias. (Haydock) — He is more attentive to the house of David, and to display the rewards of the piety, and the punishment of vice, as well as the glory of the temple and of religion, than to describe the military exploits, which occupy so much of the profane history. (Calmet)
