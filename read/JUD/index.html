Jude

1 chapter

At a glance

Author

'Jude, the servant of Jesus Christ, and brother of James' (verse 1). Most Catholic commentators, with Challoner, identify him with the apostle Jude, also called Thaddeus, 'Jude the brother of James' in Luke 6:16; the 1913 article calls this most highly probable. Hegesippus, cited there, says Jude was a relative of the Lord according to the flesh.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistle of St. Jude”, I.(1) Jude in the Books of the New Testament↗Challoner↗

Date

Disputed. The 1913 article argues it was written before A.D. 70 and before 2 Peter, so shortly before 64-65; Challoner and Haydock call the time uncertain but infer from verse 17 that few apostles, perhaps only John, were then still living.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistle of St. Jude”, V. Date and Place of Composition↗Challoner↗Haydock (1859)↗

Setting

Unknown; the 1913 article prefers Palestine, probably Jerusalem, while admitting it can only guess.

About this book

Audience

Those 'beloved in God the Father, and preserved in Jesus Christ, and called' (verse 1), whom the 1913 article takes to be Jewish Christians of Palestine or a nearby land rather than the Gentile churches of Asia Minor.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistle of St. Jude”, IV. To Whom Addressed↗

Purpose

To warn against ungodly men who had crept in, turning grace into license and denying the Lord, and to urge the faithful to contend for 'the faith once delivered to the saints' (verse 3). Haydock names the Simonians, Nicolaites and Gnostics.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistle of St. Jude”, III. Occasion and Object↗Challoner↗Haydock (1859)↗

Key themes

  • Contend for the faith once delivered to the saints (verse 3)
  • Examples of God's judgment: Israel in the desert, the fallen angels, Sodom (verses 5-7)
  • Michael the archangel's restraint toward the devil (verse 9)
  • Enoch's prophecy against the ungodly (verses 14-15)
  • Remember the apostles' warnings; build yourselves up and pray in the Holy Spirit (verses 17-21)
  • Mercy toward those who waver, and a closing doxology (verses 22-25)

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistle of St. Jude”, II. Analysis of the Epistle↗

Structure

An opening with greeting and purpose (1-4); a first part against the false teachers, their life and their coming judgment (5-16); a second part of exhortation to the faithful (17-23); and a doxology (24-25).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistle of St. Jude”, II. Analysis of the Epistle↗Haydock (1859)↗

How the Church reads it

The Catechism takes verse 3, 'the faith once for all delivered to the saints', to describe the faith that the Church guards and hands on and to which the whole People of God unfailingly holds (CCC 93, 171, 785). Haydock reads its warnings against early and later heresies as a witness that the Church endures. The 1913 article explains that its use of material found in Enoch and in a tradition about Moses (verses 9, 14-15) does not make those books Scripture.

In the canon

One of the seven New Testament books once disputed. Eusebius counted it among the contested books though read in many churches; it was received everywhere in the West by the early fifth century, and Trent lists 'one of Jude the apostle'.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistle of St. Jude”, I.(2) Tradition as to the Genuineness and the Canonicity↗Haydock (1859)↗Council of Trent, Session IV↗

The old texts and the sources

Haydock's preface (1859 text)
This Epistle, as we find by Euseb. (l. iii. Hist. c. xxv.) and S. Jerom, (in Catal.) was not everywhere received as canonical till about the end of the fourth age. It is cited by Origen, hom. vii. in Josue; by Tertul. l. de cultu fœminarum; by Clem. Alex. l. iii. Pædag.; by S. Athan. in Synopsi; by S. Greg. Naz. Carm. xxxiv.; by S. Cyr. of Jerusalem, Catech. 4ta.; by the councils of Laodicea and the third council of Carthage; by S. Aug. l. ii. de Doct. Christianâ, c. viii. See Tillemont, and Nat. Alex. in his preface to this epistle. The time when it was written is uncertain, only it is insinuated v. 17 that few of the apostles were then living, perhaps only S. John. The design was to give all Christians a horror of the detestable doctrine and infamous practices of the Simonites, Nicolaites, and such heretics, who having the name of Christians, were become a scandal to religion and to all mankind, as may be seen in S. Irenæus and S. Epiphanius. He copies in a manner what S. Peter had written in his third Epistle, C. ii. (Witham) — S. Jude in the first part of his Epistle, (v. 1 to 16) writes against certain heretics of his day, known in history by the name of Gnostics, whose extravagant opinions and shameful and criminal disorders have been described by S. Epiphanius, S. Irenæus, and other Fathers. In the second part, he seems to have principally in view such as were to arise in the latter times; and he exhorts such of the faithful as should live to see those days, to remain firm in the faith which they had received, applying themselves to prayer, persevering in charity, and awaiting the mercy of our Lord Jesus Christ and eternal life, which He has promised them. S. Jude in thus exerting himself, like S. Peter, against the first and last heresies, has invincibly established the perpetuity of the Catholic Church. With regard to the doubts of certain authors relative to the authenticity of this Epistle, we can oppose Origen, who says that S. Jude wrote a letter, which in the few lines it contains, includes discourses full of force and heavenly grace — Ioudas egrapsen epistolen oligostichon men, pepleromenen de ton tes ouraniou charitos erromenon logon. — And S. Epiphanius says, that he believed the Holy Ghost inspired S. Jude with the design of writing against the Gnostics in the letter he has left us. . . . We find it inserted in the ancient catalogues of sacred Scripture, as in that of the council of Laodicea, can. lx; of Carthage, can. xlvii: nor can there be any reasonable doubt at present for admitting it into the canon of Scripture. It is received by the Catholic Church, and has been received ever since the fourth age. What gave doubts relative to the authenticity of this Epistle, was the author's quoting a prophecy of Enoch, which seemed to have been taken from a spurious work published under the name of this patriarch, and a fact concerning the death of Moses, not found in the canonical books of the Old Testament; but the apostle might have cited the prophecy of Enoch, and the fact concerning Moses, on the faith of some ancient tradition, without a reference to any book. Eusebius (Hist. Eccles. l. iii. c. xxv.) bears testimony that this Epistle, though not frequently cited by the ancients, was publicly read in many Churches. Clement of Alexandria, Tertullian, and the later Fathers, have admitted it as a part of canonical Scripture. Hence Luther, the Centuriators of Magdeburg, and the Anabaptists, have no just reason to look upon this Epistle as doubtful. Le Clerc, in his Hist. Eccles. (an. 90.) acts more candidly in admitting it without any scruple. As for the exception Grotius takes from S. Jude not assuming the quality of apostle, and from its not being universally received in the first ages, we can answer, that S. Peter, S. Paul, S. John, did not take the title of apostles at the head of all their letters, and that some Churches have doubted at first of the authenticity of other writings, which have afterwards been universally acknowledged as authentic and canonical.
Challoner's introduction (1749–52)
St. Jude, who wrote this Epistle, was one of the twelve Apostles and brother to St. James the Less. The time it was written is uncertain: only it may be inferred from verse 17 that few or none of the Apostles were then living, except St. John. He inveighs against the heresies and wicked practices of the Simonians, Nicolaites, and Gnostics, etc., describing them and their leaders by strong epithets and similes, He exhorts the faithful to contend earnestly for the faith first delivered to them and to beware of heretics.
