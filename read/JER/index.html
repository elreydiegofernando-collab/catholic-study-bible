Jeremiah

Douay: Jeremias · 52 chapters

At a glance

Author

Jeremias, a priest of Anathoth called young in 627 B.C., who prophesied for about forty years. The 1913 article draws on chapter 36: he dictated his discourses to his scribe Baruch in 604 B.C., and after the king burned the scroll a fuller edition was made; it thinks later additions came from Baruch or another prophet, chapter 52 being taken from 4 Kings.

Date

From the call in the thirteenth year of Josias (627 B.C.) to some time after the fall of Jerusalem (586), the last oracles being given in Egypt; the first collection was written down in 604 B.C.

Setting

Jerusalem in Juda's last decades, under Josias, Joakim and Sedecias, as Babylon rose; after the city fell, Masphath, and finally the Jewish colonies in Egypt.

About this book

Audience

Kings, priests and people of Juda who would not hear him, and the exiles to whom he wrote; Haydock adds that he was a prophet of the Gentiles as well as of the Jews.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Jeremias (the Prophet)”, II. Mission of Jeremias↗Haydock (1859), Preface↗

Purpose

The 1913 article says his first task was to proclaim the certainty of the catastrophe of 586, and his second to show it as a moral necessity, the fruit of the nation's guilt since Manasses. Beyond the judgment he announces restoration after seventy years and a new covenant written on the heart (31:31-34).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Jeremias (the Prophet)”, II. Mission of Jeremias↗CCC 64↗

Key themes

  • The prophet called before birth and set over nations (1:5).
  • True and false prophecy; the cost of speaking God's word.
  • Judgment on Jerusalem for idolatry and injustice.
  • The new covenant written on the heart (31:31-34).
  • Hope after seventy years of exile.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Jeremias (the Prophet)”, V. The Book of the Prophecies of Jeremias, A. Analysis of Contents↗CCC 64↗CCC 715↗CCC 2584↗

Structure

The 1913 article finds two main divisions: discourses against Juda mixed with personal and national narratives (1-45), and oracles against nine heathen nations (46-51), with chapter 52, a historical appendix taken from 4 Kings. It notes a broadly chronological order: 1-6 under Josias, 7-20 largely under Joakim, 21-33 partly under Sedecias, 34-39 the siege, 40-45 after the fall.

How the Church reads it

The Catechism reads Jeremias's promise of a new covenant written on hearts as central to the prophets' hope (CCC 64, 715), and takes his 'one to one' prayer with God as a model of the prophets' prayer (CCC 2584). Challoner calls him a special figure of Jesus Christ in his persecutions, his charity toward his persecutors and his violent death; the 1913 article notes the tradition, first in Tertullian, that he was stoned in Egypt, and that the Roman Martyrology keeps his memory on 1 May.

In the canon

A protocanonical book; Trent's list names 'Jeremias, with Baruch' (Session IV), and the Catechism counts Jeremiah and Lamentations as one book in one way of reckoning the forty-six (CCC 120).

The old texts and the sources

Haydock's preface (1859 text)
Jeremias was a priest, a native of Anathoth, a priestly city, in the tribe of Benjamin, and was sanctified from his mother's womb to be a prophet of God; which office he began to execute when he was yet a child in age. He was in his whole life, according to the signification of his name, great before the Lord, and a special figure of Jesus Christ, in the persecutions he underwent for discharging his duty, in his charity for his persecutors, and in the violent death he suffered at their hands; it being an ancient tradition of the Hebrews, that he was stoned to death by the remnant of the Jews who had retired into Egypt, (Challoner) at Taphnes. His style is plaintive, (Worthington) like that of Simonides, (Calmet) and not so noble as that of Isaias and Osee. (St. Jerome) — He was the prophet of the Gentiles, as well as of the Jews, predicting many things which befell both, and particularly the liberation of the latter, A. 3485, after the seventy years' captivity, dating from A. 3415, (Calmet) or 3398, the 4th of Joakim. Usher C. xxv. (Haydock) — He began to prophesy when he was very young, A. 3375, in the 13th year of Josias, (Calmet) before that prince had brought his reformation to any great perfection. (Haydock)
