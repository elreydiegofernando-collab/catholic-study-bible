1 Peter

5 chapters

At a glance

Author

Peter the apostle (1:1), who calls himself a witness of Christ's sufferings (5:1) and Mark his son (5:13). The early Church received it as his without dispute; the 1913 article thinks it probable that Silvanus, through whom Peter says he wrote (5:12), helped shape the Greek, though his share cannot be fixed.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles of Saint Peter”, First Epistle: A. Authenticity↗Haydock (1859)↗Challoner↗

Date

The sources range widely. The 1913 article thinks the end of 63 or start of 64 most probable, after Ephesians and before Nero's persecution; Witham, in Haydock, reports many putting it before 49 and others after 60, and Challoner gives about fifteen years after the Ascension.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles of Saint Peter”, First Epistle: C. Place and Date of Composition↗Haydock (1859)↗Challoner↗

Setting

Rome, which Peter calls 'Babylon' (5:13), as Christian antiquity and most critics agree (1913 article, Challoner, Haydock).

About this book

Audience

The Christians of Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia and Bithynia (1:1). The 1913 article judges them mostly of pagan origin, the 'strangers' being Christians in exile from their heavenly home; Origen and Didymus thought them converted Jews.

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles of Saint Peter”, First Epistle: B. Recipients↗

Purpose

To confirm them in faith, console them in trials, and show them how to live among unbelievers and suffer as Christ did, so that even their accusers would come to glorify God (1913 article; Haydock).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles of Saint Peter”, First Epistle: B. Occasion and Object↗Haydock (1859)↗

Key themes

  • New birth to a living hope through Christ's Resurrection (1:3-12)
  • Be holy; redeemed by the precious blood of Christ (1:13-21)
  • Living stones, a royal priesthood, a holy nation (2:4-10)
  • Good conduct among pagans; duties to rulers, masters and spouses (2:11-3:7)
  • Suffering for doing good, after Christ, who preached to the spirits in prison (3:13-22)
  • Baptism prefigured by Noah's ark (3:20-21)
  • Presbyters as shepherds of God's flock (5:1-4)

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles of Saint Peter”, First Epistle: D. Analysis↗

Structure

Address and thanksgiving for salvation (1:1-12); a call to Christian holiness (1:13-2:10); rules of conduct among pagans and in suffering (2:11-4:19); counsels to presbyters and faithful (5:1-11); and an epilogue naming Silvanus and sending greetings from Rome and Mark (5:12-14).

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), “Epistles of Saint Peter”, First Epistle: D. Analysis↗

How the Church reads it

The Catechism reads 2:5 and 2:9 of the common priesthood of all the baptized (CCC 1141, 1546), 3:18-19 of Christ's descent among the dead (CCC 632), 3:21 of baptism foreshadowed by the ark (CCC 1094), and 1:18-20 of redemption by Christ's blood according to God's plan (CCC 602). Challoner and Haydock see in its tone the authority of the Prince of the Apostles.

In the canon

Protocanonical; Eusebius placed it among the undisputed books (1913 article). Trent lists 'two of Peter the apostle'.

The old texts and the sources

Haydock's preface (1859 text)
This first Epistle of S. Peter, though brief, contains much doctrine concerning faith, hope, and charity, with divers instructions to all persons of every state and condition. The apostle commands submission to rulers and superiors, and exhorts all to the practice of a virtuous life, in imitation of Christ. This epistle was written with such apostolical dignity, as to manifest the supreme authority with which its writer, the prince of the apostles, had been invested by his Lord and Master, Jesus Christ. He wrote it at Rome, which figuratively he calls Babylon, about fifteen years after our Lord's ascension. (Challoner) — S. Peter, otherwise called Simon, son of John or Jonas, was from Bethsaida, a city of Galilee. He was married, and lived at Capharnaum, and was employed with his brother Andrew, as fishermen, when our Lord called them. S. Peter on every occasion testified a more than usual zeal for his Master, and hence our Lord shewed him a very particular and very marked attention. He would have Peter present at his transfiguration; (Luke ix. 28.) and at another time declared that he was a rock, upon which he would build his Church, against which the gates of hell should never prevail. Mat. xvi. 18. Although S. Peter had the misfortune or weakness to deny Jesus Christ in his passion, our Lord, after his resurrection, gave him fresh proofs of his regard. Mat. xvi. 7. He continued him in his primacy over all, and appointed him in the most explicit manner visible head of his Church, when thrice asking Peter: "lovest thou me more than these?" and S. Peter as often answering, Christ said to him: "feed my lambs, feed my sheep." John xxi. 15. — This epistle was always received in the Church as canonical, and as written by S. Peter, prince of the apostles. It is commonly agreed that it was written from Rome, which S. Peter calls Babylon, (C. v. 13.) and directed to those in the provinces of Pontus, Galatia, &c. (v. 1.) who were before Jews or Gentiles, and had been converted to the Christian faith. It is certain this letter was not written till after the true believers had the name of Christians. C. iv. 16. Many think it was written before the year 49; but this is not certain. Others judge not till after the year 60, and some not till a short time before he wrote his second epistle. See Tillem. t. i. Art. 31. on S. Pet. and tom. ii. on S. Mark, p. 89. The main design is to confirm the new converts in the faith of Christ, with divers instructions to a virtuous life. (Witham) — Grotius, Erasmus, and Estius, discover in this epistle, a strength and majesty worthy the prince of the apostles. Est autem epistola profecto digna Apostolorum Principe, plena authoritatis et majestatis Apostolicæ: verbis parca, sententiis referta.
Challoner's introduction (1749–52)
The first Epistle of St. Peter, though brief, contains much doctrine concerning Faith, Hope, and Charity, with divers instructions to all persons of what state or condition soever. The Apostle commands submission to rulers and superiors and exhorts all to the practice of a virtuous life in imitation, of Christ. This Epistle is written with such apostolical dignity as to manifest the supreme authority with which its writer, the Prince of the Apostles, had been vested by his Lord and Master, Jesus Christ. He wrote it at Rome, which figuratively he calls Babylon, about fifteen years after our Lord’s Ascension.
